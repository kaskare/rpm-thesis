What a Symmetry Is Worth. Pricing Inductive Bias in Relational Models of Raven's Matrices.

Working title. All chapters drafted so far, as of 23 November 2026, in a version for listening: tables, template code, references and the notes for the author are left out.

Abstract.

Neural networks that read the images directly can solve generated Raven's Progressive Matrices, but only with a great deal of data: a vision transformer trained on I-RAVEN answers 19% of puzzles from 1,000 training puzzles per layout and 93% from 6,000. This thesis studies the alternative, structure built in before training, and asks what a built-in symmetry is worth to a relational model, and whether that can be known before training.

The models are lifted relational neural networks in PyNeuraLogic, where a template of weighted rules is grounded into a different network for every puzzle. We prove that a template cannot tell apart constants it does not name, so a symmetry is built in by leaving the transformed things unnamed, and holds exactly for every setting of the weights. With this method we build a model that generates the missing panel from a graph of the puzzle's objects, without being given the rules or seeing the answer candidates. On the I-RAVEN test set it reaches 97.2% from object labels and 97.0% end to end behind an object recognizer trained on the labelled objects of every panel. The vision transformer, which is given the label of the answer only, reaches 93.1% with six times the weights. Given the same labels, a black-box transformer over the recognizer's output needs about ten times the training puzzles to reach the 90% the facet model gets from 300 per layout.

We then put a price on symmetries. Counting the orbits of a rule's table under the model's symmetry predicts how much data the symmetry saves, and the ceiling it sets when the rule does not share it. Two rules matched in form and size, sum and midpoint, test this. From 600 puzzles a shift-equivariant model that reads the answer off the question row reaches 91% on midpoint and 6% on sum, whose predicted ceiling is 7.9%; given addition as background knowledge, it learns sum from 100 puzzles. On the benchmark, removing the shift symmetry costs 61 points of accuracy at 100 training puzzles and 2 at 6,000. Counted before the runs, the symmetry between the attributes of an object is worth two to three times the training data, and the model without it needs that much more. A symmetry that conflicts with the data holds the model near the ceiling computed in advance. The counts bound what a network can reach; whether it gets there also depends on how it reads the puzzle, and the thesis reports where it falls short.

Keywords: Raven's Progressive Matrices, inductive bias, symmetry, equivariance, lifted relational neural networks, graph neural networks, neuro-symbolic learning, sample efficiency

Chapter 1. Introduction.

Section 1.1. Motivation.

A Raven's Progressive Matrix shows eight panels arranged in a three-by-three grid, with the ninth left empty. Each panel holds simple figures, and the figures change along the rows according to rules the solver is not told: a shape stays the same, a size grows by one step, a colour is the sum of the two before it. The solver has to find the rules in the two complete rows, apply them to the third, and pick the missing panel from eight candidates. Raven (1938) designed the test to measure reasoning that does not depend on language or schooling, and the rules behind its items were later analysed by Carpenter et al. (1990).

Machine learning took over the format through generated datasets. In PGM (Barrett et al., 2018) and RAVEN (Zhang et al., 2019a) a program produces every puzzle. For RAVEN it picks one of seven layouts, gives each attribute of the objects (type, size, colour, number and position) one of four rules (Constant, Progression, Arithmetic, Distribute Three), fills the rows accordingly and renders the panels. I-RAVEN (Hu et al., 2021) repaired the way RAVEN draws its wrong answers, and I-RAVEN-X (Hersche et al., 2024) lengthened the rows to ten panels and widened the value ranges up to a thousand. These three are the datasets of this thesis.

The first neural solvers were black boxes: networks that map the sixteen images of a puzzle to the index of the answer (Barrett et al., 2018; Zhuo and Kankanhalli, 2020). Two things about them motivate this work.

The first is that they find shortcuts. In RAVEN every wrong candidate differs from the answer in one attribute, so the answer is the candidate that most resembles the others, and a model that never looks at the matrix can find it (Hu et al., 2021). In our own check, a model that sees only the eight candidates scores 90 to 100% on RAVEN and is at chance on I-RAVEN. A high score on such a benchmark may therefore say little about reasoning, which is the general problem of shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020).

The second is that they need a great deal of data. We trained a vision transformer on I-RAVEN to generate the answer from the image of the matrix. With 300 training puzzles per layout it answers 16% of unseen puzzles, where guessing gives 12.5%. With 1,000 it answers 19%, with 3,000 it answers 46%, and only with 6,000 does it reach 93%. The information is in the small sets all the same: a model that is given the objects of each panel, the facet model of Chapter 4, reaches 90% from the same 300 puzzles. It still reaches 90% when an object recognizer, trained on the labelled panels of those 300 puzzles, reads the images for it, although that recognizer learns from far more labels than the transformer, which sees only the answer.

Chollet (2019) argues that this is the number that matters. Skill at a fixed task can be bought with unlimited training data or with unlimited prior knowledge, so skill alone says little about a system; what is informative is how much experience it needed, given the priors it started with. We take this as a rule for reporting. Every model in this thesis states what is built into it, and its accuracy is reported against the number of training puzzles.

What can be built in is the subject of a long argument. One side holds that general methods and more computation overtake hand-built structure in the end (Sutton, 2019). The other holds that structure which matches the problem, relational structure in particular, is what lets a model generalise from little data (Battaglia et al., 2018). Geometric deep learning gives the second view a precise form: the structure to build in is a symmetry, a set of transformations of the input under which the answer does not change or changes in a known way (Bronstein et al., 2021), and there are proofs that a model with the right invariance generalises strictly better (Elesedy and Zaidi, 2021) and, for kernel methods, needs fewer examples by up to the size of the group (Bietti et al., 2021; Tahmasebi and Jegelka, 2023).

In practice, though, a prior is still chosen by intuition and judged afterwards by the accuracy it gives. That leaves two questions unanswered each time. How many training examples did the prior replace? And what would it have cost had it been the wrong one? A prior that fits its benchmark looks the same, in a results table, as a prior that is right in general.

Section 1.2. The question and the approach.

This thesis asks: what is a built-in symmetry worth to a relational model, and can that be known before training?

Raven's matrices suit the question for a reason that is usually held against them. The data is generated, so its symmetries are known exactly. The two complete rows of a puzzle can be exchanged. In a layout with two groups of objects, the groups can be exchanged. Size and colour follow the same rules and can be exchanged. Most rules survive when every value is shifted by the same amount, and one does not. Some of these transformations turn a valid puzzle into another puzzle the generator could have produced, some into a valid puzzle it never produces, and some into a puzzle whose right answer is a different one. A symmetry can thus be the right one, a harmless one or a wrong one (correct, extrinsic and incorrect equivariance in the terms of Wang et al., 2023a), and which it is can be read off the data.

The models are lifted relational neural networks (Šourek et al., 2021), written in PyNeuraLogic. Such a model is a template: a set of weighted rules over variables. For each puzzle the template is grounded on the facts that describe that puzzle, and the result is a neural network whose shape follows the puzzle: more objects give more neurons, but the weights belong to the rules and are the same everywhere. Like a graph neural network (Zhou et al., 2020), the template shares its weights over a structure; unlike one, it lets the designer say which structure, rule by rule. Whatever a rule refers to only through a variable, it treats alike. So in a template a symmetry is a decision about what to name.

Our claim is that the worth of such a decision can be computed. A symmetry merges the inputs it maps onto one another into orbits, and a model that has the symmetry built in must treat all inputs of an orbit alike. If the rule to be learned treats them alike too, the model has only as many things to learn as there are orbits, and the number of orbits measures the data it needs. If the rule asks for something different on each input of an orbit, the model can be right on at most one of them, and the number of orbits over the number of inputs is a ceiling on its accuracy that no amount of data lifts. Both numbers come from counting, before any model is trained.

Section 1.3. Contributions.

A method for building symmetries into a relational model. We prove that the output of a template does not change when constants that it does not name are renamed throughout a puzzle's facts (Chapter 3). The statement is the familiar invariance of relational models under isomorphism and its proof is short; what we add is its use as one test that every symmetry in the thesis passes or fails, before any training. A transformation of the puzzle that amounts to such a renaming is then a symmetry of the model. Leaving something unnamed builds in every permutation of it, and background facts narrow that group down: facts that order the values, for instance, leave only the shifts. The symmetry is exact and holds for every setting of the weights, which we confirm on random weights to about 10⁻¹⁶. With this method we build the facet model (Chapter 4), a graph for each puzzle with one node per attribute of each object. It generates a description of the missing panel and only then picks the closest candidate, so it is never told the rules and never sees the candidates, and the shortcut above is closed by construction. On the I-RAVEN test set it answers 97.2% of puzzles from ground-truth object labels, and 97.0% end to end, when a recognizer built on the same transformer as our black-box baseline names the objects of every panel, the candidates' included (Chapter 5). On the same test puzzles the black-box transformer reaches 93.1%, with six times the weights of the whole pipeline. The two are not given the same supervision: the recognizer learns from the labelled objects of every panel, the transformer from the label of the answer only. Given the same labels, a plain transformer over the recognizer's output answers 19% from 300 puzzles per layout, where the facet model answers 90%, and needs about ten times the data to catch up (Section 5.4).

A price for a symmetry. The orbit count above gives, for a pair of model and rule, the data a symmetry saves and the ceiling a mismatched one sets (Chapter 3). It needs no network: a learner that only counts, one answer per orbit, realises it. It describes a learner that shares nothing across orbits, which the value models of Chapter 7 do by construction. A network reaches the count only if it can also tell the orbits apart, and Chapter 7 shows a case where it could not until the part of the model that describes a row was changed. The ceiling is the error bound of Wang et al. (2023a, Theorem 4.3) for a symmetry the task does not have, evaluated on a rule's table; the count of the data saved, and a test in which both numbers are predicted, are ours.

Measurements that test the price. The sharpest is a pair of rules built for the purpose (Chapter 7). In a row (a, b, c), sum sets c = a + b and midpoint sets c = (a + b)/2. The two have the same form and tables of nearly the same size, and models with no symmetry on the values find them equally hard. They differ in one property: adding the same number to every panel keeps a midpoint row valid and breaks a sum row. A model with that shift built in, a convolution along the axis of values, should then learn midpoint from few puzzles and be unable to learn sum, and the count says how few and how unable. From 600 puzzles the model reaches 91.0% on midpoint and 6.2% on sum, against a ceiling of 7.9% (means of three seeds, here and below). The ceiling follows from the shift together with the way the model reads the puzzle, from the question row alone: a model that measured from one row to another would not have it (Section 3.6.3). With separate weights for every value, and so without the shift, the same template reaches 18.0% and 19.4%. Trained on 100 puzzles, the model gets none of the 101 midpoint rows right that fall in an orbit absent from its training puzzles, at any seed, and about three quarters of the others. Its weights per offset make that unavoidable; the count says in advance which rows those are. And when addition itself is supplied as background facts, the same model learns to use it and gets sum from 100 puzzles (98.2%). Chapter 7 says how much that gives away: a rule's full group of moves is the rule, up to one constant. A symmetry is thus not good or bad in itself. Its worth belongs to the pair of model and rule.

The same law holds on the benchmark (Chapter 6). Removing a symmetry the data has costs most when data is scarce: 61 points for the value shift at 100 puzzles, 14 and 13 points for the symmetries between components and between attributes at 100 and 600 (three seeds), and between 0 and 6 points at 6,000. Counted in training puzzles before the runs, the components' symmetry is worth a factor of two, the size of its group, which follows almost from how the samples are formed, and the attributes' between two and three, which does not. The models without them need that much more data. A symmetry of the task that the data lacks, the order of Arithmetic's operands, still adds 7 points at 6,000 puzzles. A symmetry that maps valid puzzles to puzzles with another answer holds the model near the ceiling the count gives, about half of all values, whether it trains on 600 puzzles or on 6,000. And a model that names what it should not, here the position of a panel, learns a shortcut through it: after the two complete rows are exchanged, its accuracy on puzzles with a Distribute rule falls from 98.3% to 39.5%, while the symmetric version is unchanged.

Section 1.4. What the thesis does not claim.

It does not present the best solver of Raven's matrices. The facet model is the demonstration that the method yields a working model, not the result.

It does not claim that PyNeuraLogic makes a model more accurate. A graph neural network written in PyTorch with the same symmetries reaches 96.8% on the I-RAVEN test set, against the facet model's 97.2%. The gains reported here belong to the symmetries. What the lifted template adds is that a symmetry is declared, not engineered, and that one proof covers every symmetry built this way.

It says nothing about human intelligence. Every score is measured on puzzles from the same generator as the training puzzles, with a fixed list of attributes and with rules that run along rows only, which the tests people take do not guarantee. The rows-only prior has a price: on I-RAVEN puzzles whose matrix is transposed, so that the rules run down the columns, every facet model falls from about 97% to 45%. The priors studied here were chosen with the generator in view, and Chapter 8 separates those a person would bring to any such test from those that only this family of benchmarks rewards.

The recognizer is trained apart from the reasoning model, on the labelled objects of every panel, where the black-box model is given labels for the answer only. Chapter 5 states what each model is given.

It does not claim that our black-box baseline is the best vision model. It is a plain vision transformer trained from scratch. At the same amount of data, three published architectures for these puzzles, SCL, PredRNet and DRNet, reach 83 to 91% when retrained under one protocol, and 95 to 98% as their authors report them (Małkiński and Mańdziuk, 2025a). They pick among the candidates, where ours generates the answer. Section 5.1 sets the numbers side by side.

The comparisons with little data in Chapters 6 and 7 have three seeds; most other numbers come from a single training run, and the text says which.

Section 1.5. The assignment.

The assignment asks for symmetry-aware neural architectures that combine object recognition with inductive priors on the logical composition of the objects, built on graph or relational neural networks, and for an improvement on black-box vision models. Chapters 3 and 4 give the architectures and the priors, and Section 4.3 the object recognition. Chapter 5 gives the comparison with a black-box vision model on the same task, where the gain is largest when training puzzles are few. Chapters 6 and 7 measure what each prior contributes.

Chapter 2. Background and related work.

Section 2.1. Raven's matrices and the three datasets.

The format. A Raven's Progressive Matrix, in its best-known form, is a three-by-three grid of panels with the last one missing. The figures change along the rows by rules the solver has to find, and the answer is chosen from eight candidates (Raven, 1938).

RAVEN. Zhang et al. (2019a) generate such puzzles by program. A puzzle has one of seven layouts: a single centred object, a 2 by 2 grid, a 3 by 3 grid, two objects side by side (left-right), two objects one above the other (up-down), one object inside another (in-out), and a 2 by 2 grid inside an object (in-out grid). The last four layouts have two components, each with its own rules. Every object has a type, a size and a colour, with 5, 6 and 10 values, and a grid component also has the number of its objects and their positions. The object's orientation follows no rule and acts as a distractor.

I-RAVEN. RAVEN builds each wrong candidate by changing one attribute of the answer. The answer is then the candidate that shares the most attribute values with the others, and a model can find it without looking at the matrix. Hu et al. (2021) showed this and regenerated the candidates so that the vote is balanced. In our own check, a model given only the eight candidates scores 90–100% on RAVEN and is at chance on I-RAVEN (Chapter 4). All I-RAVEN experiments here use 10,000 puzzles per layout.

I-RAVEN-X. Hersche et al. (2024) extend the single-object layout in two directions: rows of n panels instead of three, and values from 0 to m − 1, with m up to 1,000, instead of about ten values. Arithmetic then sums or subtracts n − 1 operands, and a model that stores sums has far more of them to store. I-RAVEN-X has no images; a puzzle is its attribute values. The generator's Distribute rule can also draw m itself, so the data hold values from 0 to m, and we name the datasets by that range: rows of 3 with values 0–10, and rows of 10 with values 0–50, 0–100 and 0–1,000. Figures 2.1 and 2.2 show a puzzle from each.

What generated puzzles are not. The tests people take consist of a few dozen hand-made items, ordered from easy to hard, with no training set. The generated datasets keep the format and part of the rule list. In the RAVEN family the rules run along rows only, the list of attributes is fixed, and training and test puzzles come from one program. We measured how much depends on the row direction: after transposing an I-RAVEN matrix, 3% of Progression instances and 1% of Arithmetic instances still satisfy their rule, against 100% for Distribute Three. Barrett et al. (2018) themselves call their generated task a different problem from the psychometric test. We therefore draw no conclusion about human reasoning from any number in this thesis, and return to what the generator contributes in Chapter 8.

Section 2.2. Graph neural networks.

A graph neural network keeps a vector for every node of a graph and updates it in rounds: each node collects messages from its neighbours, combines them with an operation that does not depend on their order, such as a sum or a mean, and passes the result through a learned function (Zhou et al., 2020). The same functions serve every node and every edge. Two consequences matter here.

First, the network accepts graphs of any size, because its weights belong to the update rule and not to particular nodes. Second, renumbering the nodes changes nothing: the output of a node moves with the node. This permutation symmetry is the one symmetry every graph network has by construction. Battaglia et al. (2018) describe the general point: an architecture expresses assumptions about the data, which they call relational inductive biases, and a graph network assumes that entities and their relations matter and their order does not.

What a plain graph network does not decide is everything else: which things are nodes, which edges exist, and which labels the nodes carry. A label such as "row 1" or "the left component", given to a node as an input feature, lets the network treat that row or component differently from the others. Chapter 4 uses this. Two of our baselines are graph networks on the same graph, and differ only in whether nodes carry such absolute labels.

Edges join two nodes. A relation among three things, such as u + w = v among three numbers, is a hyperedge, and a network that passes messages along pairs has to be extended to use it.

Lifted relational neural networks (Šourek et al., 2018, 2021) describe a model as a template: a set of rules in the style of logic programming, with weights attached. A rule such as

Grounding. For each sample, the template and the facts are grounded: every way of substituting the sample's constants for a rule's variables that makes the rule's body derivable becomes one copy of the rule. The copies form a computation graph, with a neuron for each fact, each ground rule body, each aggregation over the bodies that share a head, and each derived atom (Šourek et al., 2021, Section 3.2). A different sample gives a different network. The weights do not change: in the authors' words, they are shared "in a scheme determined by the lifted structure of the template". Variables that occur only in the body of a rule are aggregated over, which corresponds to quantifying over them. Figure 2.3 shows one template grounded on two puzzles.

The two ground networks have different nodes and edges, but every edge carries one of the template's weights: V from a facet to its panel, W1 to W3 from the panels to the row, and U_ab between objects at the same position in columns a and b.

Relation to graph networks. A message-passing layer is one such rule over edge facts, and Šourek et al. (2021) reproduce standard graph networks as templates. Templates go further in three ways that this thesis uses: rules over relations of any arity, so a three-way relation is as natural as an edge; rules that derive new relations from the facts before any learning takes place; and recursive rules, which unfold to whatever depth the sample's structure has. The authors list attention and gating as constructs that do not fit naturally.

PyNeuraLogic is the Python front end of the NeuraLogic engine. All our relational models are written in it and trained with its own optimizer, on CPU. The engine grounds each puzzle once and keeps the ground networks in memory, which sets the cost reported in Chapter 5.

The property this thesis builds on is the sentence quoted above. Chapter 3 turns it into a theorem: a template cannot tell apart two constants that it does not name, so its output is unchanged by any renaming of such constants that maps the sample's facts onto themselves.

Section 2.4. Symmetry as inductive bias.

Bias is necessary. A learner that prefers no hypothesis over another cannot generalise beyond its training examples (Mitchell, 1980), and averaged over all problems no learner beats another (Wolpert, 1996). An advantage always comes from an assumption that fits the problem. The open question is which assumptions to make, and the field disagrees on how many: Sutton (2019) argues that general methods with more computation have overtaken hand-built knowledge again and again, while Marcus (2018) and Lake et al. (2017) argue that objects, relations and compositional structure have to be built in.

Symmetry. Let a group G act on the inputs of a function f. The function is invariant if f(g times x) = f(x) for every g in G, and equivariant if f(g times x) = g times f(x), where G also acts on the outputs. A model has a symmetry built in when this holds for every setting of its weights, and has merely learned it when it holds, approximately, after training. Geometric deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2021) reads the successful architectures this way: convolutional networks are equivariant to translations, group convolutions extend this to other groups (Cohen and Welling, 2016), networks on sets are invariant or equivariant to permutations (Zaheer et al., 2017), as graph networks are (Section 2.2), and the architecture follows from the choice of domain and group.

Symmetry is weight sharing. Ravanbakhsh et al. (2017) show that a layer is equivariant to a permutation group exactly when its weights are tied along the orbits of the group, and Kondor and Trivedi (2018) that a layer equivariant to a compact group must be a convolution over that group. Chapter 3 proves the lifted counterpart: in a template the tying is done by variables, and the group is whatever the unnamed constants and the background facts leave free.

What a symmetry is worth. A symmetry that the target function shares shrinks the set of functions the model can represent without removing the target, so fewer examples are needed. Sokolić et al. (2017) bound the generalisation error of an invariant classifier through the covering number of the base space that is left when the transformations are factored out. For T transformations that keep the transformed copies apart, this number is up to T times smaller, so the bound is up to √T smaller, which is the bound of a non-invariant classifier trained on up to T times as many examples. Elesedy and Zaidi (2021) prove a strict generalisation benefit for equivariant models (for squared loss and a symmetric input distribution, computed exactly for linear models), and Bietti et al. (2021) and Tahmasebi and Jegelka (2023) give sample-complexity gains proportional to the size of the group for kernel methods, once the sample is large enough. These results say that the gain grows with the group. They are stated for function classes and data distributions in general; they do not say what a particular symmetry is worth on a particular rule, which is what we count in Chapter 3, and, for the components of Chapter 6, in training puzzles (Section 6.2).

What a wrong symmetry costs. A model can also have a symmetry that the task lacks. Wang et al. (2023a) sort this into three cases, by what the symmetry does to the data. Equivariance is correct where the transformed input also occurs in the data and has the transformed label, incorrect where the transformed input also occurs in the data but with a different label, and extrinsic where the transformed input lies outside the data distribution. For incorrect equivariance they prove a lower bound on the error, computed orbit by orbit: an invariant model gives every input of an orbit the same label, so on each orbit it loses at least the probability mass that does not carry the orbit's commonest label (their Theorem 4.3). The bound needs no assumption that the data's distribution is symmetric, and some invariant model attains it. Their earlier paper showed that extrinsic equivariance can help (Wang et al., 2023b); this one shows that it can also hurt (their Section 5). Petrache and Trivedi (2023) analyse the trade-off between the approximation error of a misspecified symmetry and its generalisation gain. The three kinds of symmetry in our Chapter 3 (data symmetry, out of support, conflicting) are these three cases, reached from the generator's side, and the ceiling we compute for a conflicting symmetry is their bound evaluated on a rule's table. What we add is the other half of the count, the data a correct symmetry saves, and matched experiments in which both halves are predicted before training. Softer forms exist as well: a model can be given a preference for a symmetry and not a constraint (Finzi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Chapter 6 has a small result of that kind, where a model that shares all its weights but is not exactly invariant does as well as the exactly invariant one.

Shortcuts. A model without the right constraint may fit the training data by a rule that holds only there (Geirhos et al., 2020). RAVEN's candidate sets are one example. Chapter 6 shows another, in which a model that is allowed to tell panels apart learns one rule by the panel's address.

Structure that matches the computation. Xu et al. (2020) show that a network learns a task more easily when its structure mirrors the algorithm that solves it, and Xu et al. (2021) that a graph network's success in extrapolating algorithmic tasks relies on the task's non-linearities being built into the architecture or the features. Chapter 7 gives a case: a model learns sums from a hundred puzzles once addition is available as a relation, and not at all without it.

Values on a line without a zero. A symmetry that Chapter 7 turns on is the shift of all values by a constant. Stevens (1946) classifies scales by the transformations they survive: on an interval scale the zero is a convention, and only differences mean something. In geometric terms such values form an affine line, on which points can be subtracted but not added, and a weighted sum of points is defined without reference to an origin when the weights sum to one (Gallier, 2011, Lemma 2.2.1). A rule c = pa + qb therefore survives a common shift exactly when p + q = 1. The midpoint of two values satisfies this and their sum does not.

Priors and the measurement of skill. Chollet (2019) argues that skill at a task can be bought with training data or with built-in knowledge, so a score is informative only together with both. He names the priors a fair test may assume, among them objects, elementary geometry, and small numbers with addition and subtraction (in his own benchmark, below about ten), and he counts generation from one fixed program as a weakness of a benchmark, since learning the program solves every task. RAVEN is such a benchmark. We use his terms in Chapter 8 to sort our priors into those a person would bring to any such test and those read off the generator.

Section 2.5. Solvers of Raven's matrices.

Black-box image models. The first neural solvers map the images to an answer and are trained on the answer's index. WReN (Barrett et al., 2018) scores each candidate with a relation network over panel embeddings. Zhuo and Kankanhalli (2020), the reference named in this thesis's assignment, give a ResNet the sixteen panels as one stack of images and train it on the answer's index. With ImageNet pre-training a ResNet-50 answers 86% of RAVEN's test puzzles, above the human score that RAVEN reports, but it falls by 17 to 19 points when tested on layouts it was not trained on. Without answer labels, trained only on the assumption that the third row obeys the rules of the first two, a network reaches 28.5%, against 12.5% for guessing. Their results are on RAVEN, before its candidate shortcut was found. Later models add structure that matches the generator: CoPINet contrasts the candidates (Zhang et al., 2019b), SRAN encodes rows hierarchically (Hu et al., 2021), and SCL shares networks across objects, attributes and relations (Wu et al., 2020). Several of these are symmetry priors in our sense, and we credit them as such. All of them classify among the candidates. Reported accuracies on I-RAVEN reach about 98%, but when Małkiński and Mańdziuk (2025a) retrained thirteen of these models under one protocol, most scored far lower: DRNet 90.9% against a reported 97.6%, SCL 83.4% against 95.0%, STSN 59.0% against 95.7%.

Object-centric and graph models. A second group first finds the objects and then reasons over them, which is the plan the assignment asks for. Spratley et al. (2020) attend to objects before relating panels, Wang et al. (2020) build a multiplex graph over the objects of a puzzle, and Mondal et al. (2023) combine slot attention with a transformer. Our facet model belongs here, with two differences: its graph has one node per attribute of an object, not per object, and it generates the answer.

Neuro-symbolic models. PrAE (Zhang et al., 2021) recognises the objects of each panel as probability distributions over attribute values, infers which of the generator's rules each attribute follows, executes the rule to predict the answer's attributes, and picks the nearest candidate. The rules are given. It is trained on the answer's index, through a choice among the eight candidates, with the rules of each training puzzle as an auxiliary target. This is the closest precedent for our task of generating the answer and then matching, and for the symbolic reference models of Chapter 4. ALANS (Zhang et al., 2022) removes the given rules by representing values as matrices and learning operators on them. NVSA (Hersche et al., 2023) represents attributes as high-dimensional vectors and writes each rule as an expression in vector-symbolic algebra; ARLC (Camposampiero et al., 2024) learns such expressions from data. In these models addition is an operation of the representation, so they compare with our models that are given addition, not with those that have to learn sums (Chapter 7).

Language models. Given the attribute values as text, large language models solve many of these puzzles without training on them. Hersche et al. (2024) report that GPT-4, given the attribute values as text, answers 93% of I-RAVEN's centre puzzles and 77–82% of I-RAVEN-X puzzles with rows of 10, and that on Arithmetic at range 50 it reaches 30% where the learned ARLC reaches 82%.

Arithmetic in neural networks. Networks that are not given addition learn it poorly. Trask et al. (2018) find that networks trained on sums fail outside the trained range and describe the failure as memorisation. Small transformers trained on modular addition first memorise and, much later, generalise (Power et al., 2022), by representing each number as an angle so that adding becomes rotating (Nanda et al., 2023). Mohamadi et al. (2024) prove that a permutation-equivariant kernel method, which is how a network behaves early in training, must see a constant fraction of the table of modular addition before it generalises. That is the situation of a model with separate weights for every value, and in Chapter 7 such a model tracks a learner that only memorises.

Section 2.6. Where this thesis stands.

Three things separate this work from the solvers above.

The question is the price of a prior, not the score. The models above each add structure and report accuracy. We take one model family and add or remove one symmetry at a time, at several amounts of training data, and compare each result with a number computed before training.

The symmetry is declared. In a template a symmetry is the absence of a name, and one theorem covers every symmetry built this way. The architectures above are each engineered for their symmetry, and each needs its own argument.

The answer is generated and the candidates are never seen, in training or as input. Only PrAE and its successors also generate the answer before matching, and they are given the rules or the operations; PrAE is still trained through a choice among the candidates.

Chapter 3. Symmetry in a lifted template.

This chapter holds the theory of the thesis. Its first half explains how a symmetry gets into a lifted template and why it then holds exactly. A template cannot tell apart constants that it does not name (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). What the template names and which background facts it is given decide, together, which group the model has (Section 3.3). The same theorem shows two different ways of taking a symmetry out again (Section 3.4). Section 3.5 sorts symmetries by what they do to the puzzles a generator produces. The second half puts a price on a symmetry before any training: the data it saves and the ceiling it sets (Sections 3.6 and 3.7).

None of the proofs is long. What the chapter adds is that every statement is tied either to a check that needs no training or to a count that was made before the runs of Chapters 6 and 7.

Section 3.1. Templates, samples and renamings.

We use the setting of Section 2.3. A template T is a set of weighted rules. Most arguments of its atoms are variables, but a rule may also write a constant. The rule body col(P, "c1"), for example, holds only for panels of the first column. We call the constants written in T its named constants.

A sample is a set of facts F. Here a sample is one puzzle, or one component of one puzzle (Section 4.4), and its facts are of two sorts. The puzzle facts describe what the panels show: which objects a panel holds, which component an object belongs to, and the value of each attribute. The background facts describe the frame the puzzle sits in, which is the same for every puzzle of a dataset: which panels form a row, which rows are complete, which column a panel stands in, and, for the value-axis model of Section 4.5, how the values relate to each other. Grounding T on F gives a network in which every atom a has a value. We write it val_F(a).

A renaming σ is a one-to-one map from constants to constants. It acts on a fact or an atom by renaming every constant in it, and on a set of facts one fact at a time. A transformation g of a puzzle is a renaming if some σ turns the facts of the puzzle into exactly the facts of the transformed puzzle. Exchanging rows 1 and 2 is a renaming: it swaps the panels of the two rows and the row names r1 and r2. Mirroring a left-right puzzle renames the components k0 and k1 into each other. Exchanging size and colour renames the two attributes. Not every transformation is a renaming. Moving an object to another slot of a grid changes a vector of numbers inside a fact, not a constant, and no σ does that.

As in Section 2.4, a model has a symmetry built in if f(g times x) = g times f(x) for every setting of its weights. For a renaming this has a plain meaning: the output on the transformed puzzle is the output on the original, with its atoms renamed. If no output atom contains a renamed constant, as when rows are exchanged, the symmetry is an invariance. If some do, as the output blocks for size and colour do, it is an equivariance.

Section 3.2. The renaming theorem.

Theorem. Let σ be a renaming that leaves every named constant of T in place. Then for every atom a and for every setting of the weights,

val_σ(F)(σ(a)) = val_F(a).

Proof. A ground rule is a rule r of T whose variables have been replaced by constants through a substitution θ. Since σ leaves the constants of r in place, renaming the ground rule rθ gives r(σ∘θ). That is a ground rule of the same rule r, and its body holds in σ(F) exactly when the body of rθ holds in F. The inverse of σ also leaves the named constants in place, so this pairs the ground rules of the two networks one to one. Weights belong to the rules of T and not to their groundings, so paired ground rules carry the same weights. An aggregation, such as an average or a sum, sees the multiset of values of the bodies that share a head, and σ maps this multiset one to one onto its counterpart. A product or a concatenation combines the atoms of a single body, and σ maps those one by one. For the facts, the equation holds by the definition of σ(F). The ground network has no cycles, so induction over the depth of an atom carries the equation from the facts to every atom. ∎

A transformation of the puzzle is therefore built in whenever two conditions hold:

1. it is a renaming: some σ turns the facts of the puzzle, background facts included, into exactly the facts of the transformed puzzle;

2. the template names none of the constants that σ moves.

Three remarks.

The conditions are sufficient, not necessary. A template can also be symmetric by coincidence, for instance when two rules that name different constants happen to have equal weights.

A named constant allows the symmetry to break, but does not force it. The checks below show that with random weights it does break.

The theorem is the form that the permutation equivariance of graph networks takes in a logic program. In a graph network the renamed constants are the nodes, and the only facts are edges and node features, which a permutation of the nodes maps onto themselves. In a template any constant can take that role: a row, a component, an attribute, a value. The background facts decide which renamings keep the frame of the puzzle.

The checks. Every built-in symmetry claimed in this thesis was also checked numerically, without training. With random weights, several draws and unseen puzzles, the output on the transformed puzzle is compared with the renamed output on the original (core/pynl/facet_symmetry_check.py, and the --check-shift and --check-scale options of core/pynl/value_axis.py). Where the theorem applies, the largest difference lies between 0 and 10⁻¹⁵, the size of a change in the order of a floating-point sum. Where the template names a constant that the transformation moves, the output changes by 0.4 to 1.0. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 give the individual numbers.

Section 3.3. What is named, and what the background facts keep.

The two conditions of the theorem are two places to put a symmetry. The rules decide which constants are named, and the background facts decide which renamings map a sample onto itself. Together they fix the group of the model: the renamings that leave every named constant in place and keep every background fact true. Adding a background relation can only make this group smaller, because each new fact is one more thing a renaming must keep. The background facts are therefore where a lifted template chooses its group, in the way that the choice of domain fixes the group of a convolutional or a graph network (Section 2.4).

The relations used in this thesis show how this works.

Complete rows, full(r1) and full(r2). Both complete rows carry the same fact, and the rules reach them only through it, so exchanging rows 1 and 2 keeps the frame. The question row is set apart, because the rules name it, as in inrow(P, "r3"), and no renaming may move it.

Columns, col(P, "c1"), col(P, "c2") and col(P, "c3"). The rules name each column, so no exchange of columns is built in. That is intended: a Progression row read backwards is a different row. For I-RAVEN-X with rows of ten, column roles give each panel one of the facts col(P, "first"), col(P, "mid") and col(P, "last") instead. All eight middle columns now carry the same fact, so any exchange of middle columns is built in, while the first and last columns stay apart.

Neighbours, next(P, Q). The row fold of Appendix E reads a row as a chain of neighbouring panels. Reversing a row would map next onto its inverse, which is not among the facts, so the fold keeps the direction of a row.

Offsets on the value axis, off(U, V, k) for V = U + k. In the value-axis model every value is a constant, and no rule names one. Moving all values by the same amount s maps off onto itself, exactly on a ring of values and away from the ends on a line, so the model is equivariant to this common shift.

Addition, plus(U, W, V) for U + W = V. The common shift does not keep these facts, since (u + s) + (w + s) is not (u + w) + s. Giving a model plus therefore takes the common shift out of its group. What remains are the moves that addition keeps, such as adding s to one operand and to the result.

The same mechanism lets a symmetry be stated as a relation, not only left unnamed. The two components of the left-right and up-down layouts are exchangeable because no rule names k0 or k1. One could also give the model a relation that says how the components of a layout correspond, for example a reflection with one fact per layout, and let every rule take the layout as a variable. The left-right and up-down layouts would then share every weight because no rule names either of them, and the relation would tell the model which component of one layout corresponds to which of the other. The experiments did not need this. It is the form in which the method reaches from the symmetries inside one puzzle to symmetries between layouts.

Section 3.4. Two ways to break a symmetry.

To measure what a symmetry is worth, Chapter 6 takes it out of a model and compares. The theorem has two conditions, and there is one way to break each.

Labels break the first condition. The template stays as it is, but facts tie each of the exchangeable constants to a fixed vector: cid(k0) = [1, 0] and cid(k1) = [0, 1] for the components, or a one-hot aid(A) for the attributes, read by the summary of every panel. When a puzzle is mirrored, its objects move from k0 to k1, but cid(k0) stays [1, 0]. The facts of the mirrored puzzle are then no longer a renaming of the original facts. With random weights the mirror changes the output by 0.37, and with attribute labels the exchange of type and colour changes it by 0.41. Every weight is still shared between the components or the attributes. The labels add 320 weights to the 266,528 of the components model, or 512 to the 288,328 of the attributes model.

Separate weights break the second condition. Every rule with weights is written once per component, or once per attribute, with that constant in it. The template now names the constants, and the pairing of ground rules in the proof is gone: the rules for k0 and those for k1 have weights of their own. With random weights the mirror changes the output by 0.78, and the exchange of type and colour by 0.90. At the same widths the model has twice as many weights for the components and 3.2 times as many for the attributes, so Chapter 6 also uses narrower versions with as many weights as the symmetric model.

The two ways take out different things. Labels remove the exact invariance and keep the sharing: every training puzzle still trains every weight. Separate weights remove both. Comparing a symmetric model with its labelled and its separate versions therefore tells apart what the invariance is worth and what the sharing is worth (Section 6.3).

A constant can also be named by accident. The first version of the copy links in Section 6.5 gave every panel a gate of its own, gate("p3"). That names the panels, so the exchange of rows 1 and 2 was no longer built in (0.99 with random weights), and the trained model made use of the freedom. Gates computed from what a panel contains name nothing, and with them the exchange is exact again (4.4 times 10⁻¹⁶).

Section 3.5. Three kinds of symmetry.

Whether a built-in symmetry helps or hurts depends on what it does to the puzzles that the generator produces. Take a transformation g of a complete matrix that leaves the missing panel in its place, a valid puzzle x and its answer y. There are three cases.

A data symmetry. g times x is again a valid puzzle, its answer is g times y, and the generator produces g times x as often as x. The training data then contains both, and a model with the symmetry has less to learn. This is a case of what Wang et al. (2023a) call correct equivariance; their definition asks only that g times x occur in the data, not that it occur as often. Section 3.6 counts what it is worth.

Out of support. The generator never produces g times x, so the symmetry puts no constraint on the data. This is Wang et al.'s extrinsic equivariance. For the benchmark two cases of it differ. If g times x is still a valid puzzle with the answer g times y, the symmetry belongs to the task but not to the data. Building it in costs nothing on the right solution, and it rules out solutions that hold only on what the generator produces (Section 6.5). If g times x is not a valid puzzle at all, the model never has to answer it, so no ceiling follows, but the model must find the rule in a form that respects g, and that can cost data (Section 6.7).

Conflicting. g times x is a valid puzzle that the generator does produce, but its answer is not g times y. A model with the symmetry has to answer x and g times x consistently, so it is wrong on one of them. This is Wang et al.'s incorrect equivariance, for which they prove a lower bound on the error, orbit by orbit. Section 3.6 evaluates that bound on a rule's table, and Sections 6.7 and 7.4 test it.

Wang et al. make their definitions point by point, so one move can be correct on some rules and incorrect on others, as the shift is.

Which case a transformation falls into is a property of the generator. It can be read off the generators' code and checked on the data. The table gives the share of rule instances (one attribute of one puzzle) that still satisfy a rule of the same kind after the transformation, in the order Constant / Progression / Arithmetic / Distribute. Before the transformation every instance satisfies its own rule.

Section 3.6. Pricing by orbits.

A symmetry merges inputs into orbits. This section counts what that is worth on a rule's table, in the simplest setting where the count can be made exactly: a single row of three values. First the words.

A row is three values (a, b, c). In the question row the third is missing, and a learner is anything that turns the two values (a, b) into an answer for c. We write f(a, b) for that answer.

A move changes a row in a fixed way. Three moves matter here:

the shift: add the same number s to all three values;

scaling: multiply all three values by the same number k;

one operand with the answer: add s to a and to c, or to b and to c.

A rule keeps a move if every row of the rule is again a row of the rule after the move. A learner has a move if moving its input moves its answer in the same way: for the shift, f(a + s, b + s) = f(a, b) + s. The orbit of an input is everything the moves turn it into. Values are the whole numbers 0 to 50. Where a statement divides, it is made on the ring of 101 values that the models use, where every step has a half and every multiple.

Section 3.6.1. Which rule keeps which move.

Statement 1. A rule of the form c = pa + qb keeps the shift exactly when p + q = 1. Every such rule keeps scaling. Sum keeps the move of one operand with the answer, and midpoint does not.

Why. Shift a row of the rule by s. The new third value is c + s. The rule asks for p(a + s) + q(b + s), which is pa + qb + (p + q)s, that is c + (p + q)s. The two agree for every s exactly when p + q = 1. Midpoint has ½ + ½, mirror (c = 2a − b) has 2 − 1, Progression (c = 2b − a) has −1 + 2 and Constant (c = b) has 0 + 1: all keep the shift. Sum has 1 + 1 = 2 and does not. For scaling, multiply the rule by k: kc = p(ka) + q(kb), which holds whatever p and q are. For the last move, add s to a and to c in a sum row: c + s = (a + s) + b holds; in a midpoint row the rule asks for c + s/2.

Section 3.6.2. A learner with the shift, and what it saves.

Statement 2. A learner that has the shift can only compute c = b + g(b − a), for some list of numbers g with one entry per first step b − a.

Why. Shift the input by −a. The learner must answer (0, b − a) with f(a, b) − a. So f(a, b) = a + f(0, b − a): its answer is a plus something that depends on the first step alone. Writing g(d) for f(0, d) − d gives f(a, b) = b + g(b − a). All inputs with the same first step form one orbit, and the learner has one free number for each orbit.

Statement 3 (the data saved). If the rule keeps the shift, one training row teaches the learner every row with the same first step. With rows drawn evenly from a table in which orbit o holds a share P(o) of the rows, the expected share of unseen rows whose orbit n training rows have reached is

the sum over orbits of P(o) times (1 − (1 − P(o))ⁿ).

Why. A rule that keeps the shift is itself of the form c = b + g*(b − a), by the argument of Statement 2 applied to the rule. A training row with first step d shows g*(d), and that is the right entry for every row with that first step. An unseen row is therefore answered correctly exactly when its first step occurred in training. A given orbit is missed by one training row with chance 1 − P(o) and by n independent rows with chance (1 − P(o))ⁿ. An unseen row lies in orbit o with chance P(o). Adding up over the orbits gives the formula. The same holds for any set of moves the rule keeps, with its own orbits.

Checked on midpoint. "Expected" uses the table alone. "Reached" is the counting learner on the actual training puzzles, which keeps one answer per orbit and is told the rule. 100 and 600 training puzzles hold 57 and 311 midpoint question rows.

(A table is left out here.)

The formula gives the counting learner's score to within 6 points before a single puzzle is drawn. The shift cuts the rows needed for 90% from 2,878 to 107, a factor of 27. The trained networks are within 4 points of the counted value without a symmetry. With one they are under it where many orbits are left and little data is given: by 20 points with the shift and by 5 with doubling at 100 puzzles, by 2 or less at 600, and by nothing with scaling. The three networks with a symmetry are those whose row descriptor reads the steps (Section 7.7); Section 3.7 says why that matters.

Section 3.6.3. The ceiling, and whose ceiling it is.

Statement 4 (the ceiling). On a rule in which the rows of an orbit all need different answers, a learner of the form of Statement 2 is right on at most one row of each orbit. For sum under the shift that is 97 rows of 1,225: 7.9%.

Why. On a sum row the answer is b + a, so the learner is right exactly when g(b − a) = a. Rows with the same first step have different a, and g holds one number for that step. It can match one of them. Sum's table has 97 different first steps. With rows drawn evenly, the best a learner can expect is one right row per orbit, 97 in 1,225. In general the count per orbit is the largest group of its rows whose answers agree once the move is undone; for the shift, the rows with the same c − b. Written this way a learner with the shift is a learner of c − b that ignores the shift, and the count is the bound of Wang et al. (2023a, Theorem 4.3) for such learners, evaluated here on a table.

Checked. Fitted on the whole table, the counting learner with the shift is right on 97 of 1,225 rows. With the shift and doubling it is 88 (7.2%), with the shift and scaling 17 (1.4%). The trained shared model reaches 4.5 ± 2.3 and 6.2 ± 0.8 at 100 and 600 puzzles and 7.1 at 6,000, at the epoch picked on the selection puzzles.

How sharp the check is. The ceiling is on the expected accuracy. The unseen set has 507 sum rows, on which one standard deviation of a 7.9% rate is 1.2 points. Over all 172 evaluated epochs of the nine runs, 8 are above 7.9 and the highest is 9.7, in the run on 6,000 puzzles, whose epochs average 7.9. So the model sits at the ceiling, within what chance allows on this many rows, and the measurement cannot exclude a point or two above it. Statement 5 says why a point or two would not contradict anything.

Statement 5 (the ceiling belongs to learners that answer from the question row). A model that has the shift of the whole puzzle, all nine panels together, is not capped on sum by that symmetry alone.

Why. Here is a function of the whole puzzle that uses only differences:

c₃ = b₃ + (c₁ − b₁) + (a₃ − a₁).

Shift every panel by s and the answer moves by s, so the function has the shift. On a sum puzzle c₁ − b₁ is a₁, so the function returns b₃ + a₁ + a₃ − a₁ = a₃ + b₃, the right answer. A complete sum row shows where zero is, and a learner that can measure from a panel of one row to a panel of another can use it.

Checked. The function is right on 507 of 507 unseen sum rows and follows a shift of the whole puzzle on all of them.

What this means for the trained model. Statements 2 to 4 are proved for a learner that sees the question row. The template sees the whole puzzle. It stays under the ceiling because of how it reads the puzzle, which can be read off its rules: the score of a candidate value V is computed from V's offsets to the two panels of the question row, from how many panels of each row sit exactly at V, and from a descriptor of the puzzle that is the same for every V. No rule measures the offset between a panel of the question row and a panel of another row. So apart from values that happen to coincide between rows, the template is a learner of the form of Statement 2, with a g that may depend on the descriptor. The rows of an attribute are drawn independently, so the descriptor of the complete rows says nothing about where the question row sits. For the template the ceiling is therefore a prediction that follows from its structure, and not a consequence of the puzzle-wide shift alone. The coincidences are a real way past it, though a small one: in 48 of the 507 unseen sum rows the answer is a value that already stands in the puzzle, and the template sees which values those are. The runs sit at the ceiling and not clearly above it.

In the terms of Wang et al. (2023a): shifting one row turns a sum row into a row that needs another answer, which is their incorrect equivariance, the case their lower bound covers. Shifting a whole sum puzzle turns it into something that is not a sum puzzle at all, which is their extrinsic equivariance. On such orbits only one puzzle occurs in the data and their bound is zero. Extrinsic equivariance can still hurt (Wang et al., 2023a, Section 5) or help (Wang et al., 2023b).

Section 3.6.4. More of the rule's moves.

Statement 6 (doubling). A learner that has the shift and doubling has one free number for each odd part of the first step. On the ring, a row with a first step it never saw is answerable exactly when a training row had a first step with the same odd part.

Why. By Statement 2 the learner computes b + g(d). Doubling the input must double the answer's offset from b, so g(2d) = 2g(d). Write a step as d = m times 2ʲ with m odd. Then g(d) = 2ʲ times g(m): the entry for the odd part fixes the entries for all its doublings, and one training row with any of them fixes g(m). Midpoint's even first steps between −50 and 50 have 26 odd parts.

Checked. At 100 puzzles 101 unseen midpoint rows have a new first step: 72 with an odd part that training had, 29 without. The trained doubled model is right on 50 / 43 / 39 of the 72 and on 0 / 0 / 0 of the 29, over three seeds. The model with the shift alone is right on none of the 101.

Statement 7 (scaling). On the ring, a learner that has the shift and every scaling computes c = a + t times (b − a) for one number t, on every row with a ≠ b.

Why. Any two different values (a, b) are the image of (0, 1) under "multiply by b − a, then add a". The learner has both moves, so f(a, b) = (b − a) times f(0, 1) + a. Its only free number is t = f(0, 1). Midpoint is t = ½, Progression is t = 2, mirror is t = −1.

A consequence that is also proved. With scaling built in, every question row with two different values is the same input, (0, 1). The question row then says nothing about which rule applies. Midpoint and Progression need different t, so a model with scaling can tell them apart only from the complete rows.

Checked. The counting learner with these moves is right on every midpoint row after the first training row. On sum its ceiling is 17 rows of 1,225. The consequence is what Section 7.7 measured, in both directions. With a row descriptor that came out the same for every row, the scaled model continued four midpoint rows in five as a Progression at two seeds of three. With a descriptor that reads each pair's step, it is right on all 505 midpoint rows in six runs of six, from 100 puzzles and from 600, and the descriptor separates the two rules in every run.

Statement 8 (addition's own moves). A learner that has both moves of one operand with the answer computes c = a + b + k for one number k.

Why. Move the first operand by −a and the second by −b: the learner must answer (0, 0) with f(a, b) − a − b. So f(a, b) = a + b + f(0, 0).

Checked. The counting learner with these moves is right on every sum row after one training row. The shared model given the plus facts reaches 98.2 ± 0.8 on sum from 100 puzzles and 99.7 ± 0.4 from 600.

Statement 9 (one orbit is the rule, up to one answer). If the moves a learner has can turn any input into any other, the learner is fixed by its answer to one input.

Why. Pick an input x. Every other input is some move h applied to x, and the learner must answer it with h applied to its answer to x. Nothing else is free.

This is why the number of orbits is the right unit: it counts the answers that are left to learn. Statements 7 and 8 are the two cases with one orbit, and in both the model has been handed its rule up to one number.

Section 3.6.5. Values read as numbers.

Statement 10. Let the rule be c = pa + qb, and let the two complete rows of a puzzle be (a₁, b₁, c₁) and (a₂, b₂, c₂). If a₁b₂ ≠ a₂b₁, the two rows fix p and q, a least-squares fit finds them, and its answer for the question row is exact. Nothing is learned from other puzzles.

Why. The two rows are two equations, a₁p + b₁q = c₁ and a₂p + b₂q = c₂. They have exactly one solution when a₁b₂ ≠ a₂b₁, and the rule's own p and q are a solution. Least squares returns the exact solution when there is one. When a₁b₂ = a₂b₁ the rows leave a whole line of solutions, and the fit returns the shortest, which need not be the rule's.

Section 3.6.6. Reach: what is proved and what is measured.

Statement 11. For a learner with one free number per orbit, training rows from other orbits put no condition on the number of an orbit that training never reached.

Why. By Statement 2 the learner's answer on a row depends on g at that row's first step only. A training row with another first step involves another entry of g. So every setting of the unreached entry fits the training rows equally well, and the training data cannot make it right.

Proved for: the counting learner. Measured for the networks: the kernel has one free weight vector per offset, and nothing ties the vectors of different offsets together. Section 3.7.1. What is not proved.

That the trained network reaches the count. The count is what a learner with one answer per orbit gets. The networks come within 4 points of it without a symmetry, and with the shift they stay 20 points under it at 100 puzzles and 2 at 600. A network reaches the count only if it can tell the orbits apart, which a row descriptor that pools away the steps prevents (Section 7.7). The 20 points with the shift alone may be a limit of the same kind; it is not explained here.

That a network cannot generalise past its orbits. Statement 11 is proved for the counting learner. For the networks it is a measurement on one family of templates, in which nothing but the built-in moves ties weights together. A model with other ties, such as a smooth kernel, could do otherwise.

The ceiling for the template. Statement 5: it follows from how the template reads the puzzle, with coincidences of values between rows left over. It is not a theorem about the puzzle-wide shift. The runs sit at it: 7.1 or less at every picked epoch, and above 7.9 at 8 of 172 evaluated epochs, 9.7 at the most.

Anything about groups on 0 to 50. The models compute on a ring of 101 values, chosen because 101 is prime: every step then has a half and every multiple, so the scaling moves are defined. On the values 0 to 50 the moves are not a group at all, since a shift or a scaling can leave the range. The data never leaves the part where the ring and the whole numbers agree: a midpoint row has a + b even and at most 100, so half of a + b on the ring is the ordinary midpoint. The counts are made on the actual tables. Statements 6 and 7, and the exactness checks of the scaled models (Section 7.7), hold on the ring only; on the data, scaling maps most rows out of range and is an out-of-support symmetry in the sense of Section 3.5.

Chapter 4. Task, data and models.

This chapter uses two things from Chapter 3: a template cannot tell apart constants it does not name (Section 3.2), and a symmetry is a data symmetry, out of support, or conflicting, by what it does to valid puzzles (Section 3.5).

Section 4.1. The task: generate the answer, then match it.

Most solvers of Raven's matrices score the eight candidates and are trained to give the right one the highest score. All models in this thesis do something else. They generate a description of the missing panel from the eight context panels, and only afterwards is the description compared with the candidates. The candidates are never an input and never part of the loss.

The description. A panel is described per component. For a component, the description holds, for each of type, size and colour, the mean code of its objects' values; the number of its objects; and which position slots are occupied. Values are written in a thermometer code: value v turns on the first v bits, and value 0 turns on none. On I-RAVEN the codes have 10 bits and there are 22 position slots over all layouts, so a component's description is 62 numbers between 0 and 1. On I-RAVEN-X a code has one bit per value step, 10 to 1,000 bits.

Training. A sample asks for one component of the missing panel. The loss is the squared error between the model's 62 outputs and the description of the true answer. Layouts with two components give two samples per puzzle.

Two measures.

Matched: the candidate whose description is closest to the generated one, summed over components, is the right answer. Ties count as wrong, and guessing gives 12.5%. This is the usual accuracy of the benchmark. The true description itself picks the right candidate in 99.1% of I-RAVEN puzzles and in all I-RAVEN-X puzzles.

Exact: the generated panel, decoded, equals the answer: every attribute's values, the number of objects and their positions. Exact does not use the candidates at all.

Exact is much the stricter measure, for two reasons. A model can match without knowing a value precisely, because the wrong candidates are often far away: on I-RAVEN-X with rows of 10, one of our models matches 85% and generates 15% exactly. And in the grid layouts of I-RAVEN the generator gives an attribute a rule only where the objects of a panel are uniform in it; elsewhere the values are noise that no model can predict, and exact counts them. We report matched with exact in brackets, as in 97.2 (68.4).

Generating has one consequence for the design of every model here: a shortcut through the candidate set, the flaw of RAVEN, is impossible by construction, on any dataset.

Section 4.2. Data, splits and protocol.

(A table is left out here.)

Protocol. A model is trained on the training puzzles, or on the first n of them per layout for the learning curves. Its checkpoint is the epoch with the best matched score on the selection puzzles. Its score is reported on puzzles that played no part in that choice. For I-RAVEN these are 7,000 validation puzzles, which we call unseen. They were looked at for the whole of the work, so the final I-RAVEN numbers of Chapter 5 come from the test split, which every final checkpoint was scored on exactly once, after all choices were fixed. For I-RAVEN-X the unseen puzzles are the reported ones. At the very end every final checkpoint of Chapters 5 to 7 was also scored once on puzzles 8000 to 9999 of its file, and the components ablation on the I-RAVEN test split (Appendix D.6). Those numbers agree with the reported ones within sampling error except in one single-run cell, and the text gives the test number wherever the two differ by enough to matter.

Seeds. Most results are a single run. Where two seeds exist the text gives the mean and the standard deviation; for the object-level baselines the two seeds differ by 1 to 2 points, which is the scale against which single-run differences should be read.

Checks on the data. Three checks were made before any model was trained on it. I-RAVEN's annotation lists only the occupied slots of a grid, and reading a slot from an object's index in that list, as the code we inherited did, left about a third of the grid puzzles unanswerable; slots are now derived from the bounding boxes. No puzzle occurs in two splits. And a model that sees only the eight candidates is at chance on I-RAVEN, where it scores 90–100% on RAVEN.

The symmetries the data has. Which transformations of a puzzle the generators respect was measured on the data and read off the generators' code. Section 3.5 gives the table and sorts the symmetries used in this thesis into data symmetries, symmetries out of the support and conflicting ones.

Section 4.3. Object recognition.

The relational models take the objects of each panel as input: for each object its slot, type, size and colour. During development these come from the dataset's annotation ("with XML labels"). For the end-to-end results they come from a recognizer that reads the image of one panel.

The recognizer is a vision transformer with the patch embedding and the blocks of our black-box baseline (Section 4.6): depth 6, width 64, patches of 16 pixels, 325,718 weights. In place of one answer token it has one learned query token per position slot, 22 in all, and one head shared by all slots, which says whether the slot holds an object and gives a distribution over its type, size and colour. It reads panels one at a time, at 160 by 160 pixels, and knows nothing of rows or puzzles. The 22 query tokens join the 100 patch tokens in every block, so they attend to the whole panel throughout. It was trained on the panels of 2,000 puzzles per layout, with the epoch picked on the selection puzzles.

(A table is left out here.)

By layout it reads every panel right in the centre, 2 by 2, left-right and up-down layouts, 99.92% of 3 by 3 panels, 99.99% of in-out panels and 95.56% of in-out grid panels. The small inner grid is its weak spot.

From recognizer to model. A slot becomes an object when its probability of being occupied exceeds one half, and each attribute takes its most probable value. The candidates are read by the recognizer as well, so nothing in the end-to-end pipeline comes from the annotation at test time. We also tried passing the recognizer's uncertainty on, by setting each thermometer bit to the probability that the value exceeds it. On the unseen puzzles this changed nothing (97.13% both ways), so the simpler mapping is used.

The recognizer is trained on annotated objects, apart from the reasoning model. The pipeline is end to end at test time, not in training.

Section 4.4. The facet model.

The main model is a template in PyNeuraLogic. For each puzzle it is grounded on that puzzle's facts into a network of its own.

Facts. A puzzle is described by its panels p0 … p7, the row and column of each, the objects of each panel, the component each object belongs to, and for each object and each of type, size and colour a facet: a node that carries the thermometer code of that value. Facts also say which objects of neighbouring panels sit in the same position. Rule names, arithmetic facts and candidates are not among the facts.

The forward pass. We write x_(o,a) for the thermometer code of object o's value of attribute a (type, size or colour), O(p,k) for the objects of component k in panel p, c(p) for the column of panel p, and N_j(o) for the objects in the same position as o in the panel of column j of o's row. Capital letters are learned matrices, ℓ = 1, 2 numbers the two facet layers, and the atom of the template that each line computes is in brackets (Appendix C prints the rules themselves):

Here norm(z) = z / ‖ z ‖, ρ = (p_1, p_2, p_3) is a complete row, ρ_1 and ρ_2 are the two complete rows, p_6 and p_7 the panels of the question row, and k^* the component the sample asks for. The number of objects is a fourth attribute, whose v is A applied to the code of the count, without facets. Positions follow the same row structure, starting from the occupancy of a component, the sum of its objects' one-hot slots, with matrices of their own. The output is a sigmoid over the concatenation of ŷ for the four attributes and the positions, and the loss is the squared error against the description of the true answer's component. Every matrix is shared over objects, panels, components, attributes and the two complete rows; U, W and B are indexed by columns, which the template names. The template declares a tanh on some of the linear lines above; Appendix A.2 explains why they run without it, and the equations show them as they run.

The layers have widths 32, 64 and 128, and the template has 266,528 weights. Figure 4.1 shows the graph that the template grounds to on one puzzle.

A row node reads its three panels with one weight per column (W). Section 4.5. Variants used in the experiments.

The experiments of Chapters 6 and 7 change one thing at a time. These are the changes.

A symmetry removed, in two ways (Section 6.1).

Labels. A fact gives each component, or each attribute, a fixed one-hot vector that the rules read. The weights stay shared; the model can now tell the components apart. This adds 320 or 512 weights.

Separate weights. Every rule is written once per component, or attribute, with its own weights. To keep the comparison fair this is run twice: at the same widths, which doubles or triples the weights, and at narrower widths that give the same number of weights as the shared model.

Copy links (Section 6.5). A direct connection from each context panel's value to the output, opened or closed by a learned gate. With per-panel gates each of the eight panels has its own gate weights, which names the panels and gives up the symmetry between rows 1 and 2. With content gates a gate is computed from the panel's own value with weights shared over the panels of a row, which keeps it.

Column roles (rows of 10). The columns of a row are first, middle and last, and the eight middle columns enter through one rule with the panel as a variable. The middle columns can then be exchanged, which is the symmetry of Arithmetic's operands. This variant has 288k weights against 1,411k for named columns, and is the model on which the attribute symmetry is removed in Sections 6.2 and 6.3. Section 6.6 measures what the middle columns' symmetry buys.

Two wrong symmetries (Section 6.7). Columns tied: columns 1 and 2 share their weights and enter symmetrically, so the model cannot tell (a, b, c) from (b, a, c). Values folded: v and max − v get the same input code, so the model cannot tell a puzzle from its reflection. Both are exact with random weights.

The value axis (Sections 6.2 and 7). A second template for I-RAVEN-X, built to put a symmetry on the values themselves. In the facet model a value is a code, read through one weight per bit, so "two more than 17" and "two more than 33" are different things to learn. In the value-axis template every value is a constant, v0 … v50, a panel's attribute is a fact x(P, A, v17), and background facts off(U, V, k) say that V lies k steps above U. A rule

(Template code left out here.)

is then a convolution along the line of values: the same weights at every value. No rule names a value, so the template is equivariant to shifting all values, to about 10⁻¹⁶ with random weights, and a checkpoint trained on values 0–50 runs unchanged on 0–100. Its ablation writes every rule once per value with separate weights (335k weights against 10.8k), which names the values and removes the symmetry.

This template is a test bed and not a competitor of the facet model. Besides the convolution it is given features in the shape of the rules: exact running sums along a row through a background relation plus(U, W, V) for U + W = V, the number of distinct values in a row, and offsets from the last panel. With them it answers I-RAVEN-X puzzles almost perfectly, and that says little, since the hard part was handed to it. We therefore compare the value axis only with itself: shared weights against weights per value, and with plus against without. In one variant the given sums are removed and only the plus facts are kept, so that learned rules have to use them (Chapter 7).

Section 4.6. Baselines.

Every baseline is trained on the facet model's task: the same puzzles and splits, the same target description, the same loss, the same choice of epoch and the same two measures. The trainer refuses to run unless it reproduces the facet pipeline's targets on a sample of puzzles. Only the architecture differs. Each is sized to the facet model's number of weights, about 265k on I-RAVEN.

(A table is left out here.)

A and B are the pair that separates two things the facet model changes at once: the granularity of the graph and the symmetries. Both are written in PyTorch on the facet model's graph. B has the facet model's symmetries and A does not, so the object GNN against A measures granularity, A against B measures the symmetries, and B against the facet model measures what the lifted framework itself adds.

The ViT is the black-box vision reference the assignment asks for: depth 6, pre-norm blocks, at width 64 (335k weights, the closest to the facet model) and width 224 (3,753k). It generates the same description as every other model. Its target and the candidates it is matched against come from the annotation, so it is given labels for the answer and the candidates, and images for the context.

The recognizer with a transformer is the control for supervision. The recognizer of Section 4.3, trained on the labelled panels of the same training puzzles, reads the eight context panels, and a plain transformer of depth 6 over its 8 by 22 slot states generates the answer's description. It is given what the facet model end to end is given, the same labels in training and the same perception at test time, and no structure for the task. The recognizer is either frozen, with the reasoner at width 64 (309k weights; 635k with the recognizer, against the facet pipeline's 592k) or 224 (3,661k), or trained along with its own label loss kept on the context panels. It uses the ViT's recipe.

Training. MLP, object GNN and the attribute GNNs: AdamW, 300 epochs, with the regularisation picked on the selection puzzles from a grid of 40 runs of the MLP and the object GNN (weight decay 0.5, cosine schedule, gradient clipping; layer normalisation in the MLP). The plain versions without regularisation are reported too. The ViT uses the usual recipe for a transformer trained from scratch (learning rate 5 times 10⁻⁴, weight decay 0.05, cosine schedule with warm-up, dropout 0.1), not tuned here. Larger versions of the MLP and the object GNN, up to 64 times the weights, were trained to check that size is not what they lack. Eight late runs of the ViT, made after an option for accumulating gradients was added, had a bug in the schedule and were rerun with it fixed (Appendix F.3).

Symbolic references. Two models mark the level reached when the rules are given: a template in which the four rules are written out as logic and only 41 weights are learned, and a solver in plain Python with nothing learned. They show what remains hard once the rules are known, and they are the only models here that see the candidates.

Section 5.5. The symmetries carry the result, not the framework.

The attribute GNN B is written in PyTorch and has the facet model's symmetries. It reaches 96.8% on test against the facet model's 97.2%, and stays within 3 points of it at every amount of data, ahead at 3,000. The 0.4 points on test are one run each; two seeds of the object GNN differ by 1.7. We therefore do not claim that the lifted framework makes a model more accurate. By layout, the facet model's lead is the 3 by 3 grid (89.1 against 85.5), and the two are within 0.2 points everywhere else.

What the template contributes is of another kind. The symmetries of B had to be engineered into its node labels and checked one by one. In the template they follow from which constants are left unnamed, one proof covers all of them, and removing one is a change of a line (Chapter 6).

Section 5.8. Cost.

The lifted approach has a real cost. PyNeuraLogic grounds every puzzle into its own network and keeps all of them in memory, and it updates the weights one puzzle at a time, on CPU.

Grounding takes 10 to 165 ms per puzzle and is done once; an epoch over all of I-RAVEN takes about 14 minutes. The torch baselines of the same size train on one GPU in a few gigabytes. Scoring is cheap by comparison: the whole test split, with labels and end to end, took 15 minutes and 16 GB.

Chapter 6. What a symmetry buys and what a wrong one costs.

Cells are matched % with exact % in brackets, on unseen puzzles, one seed unless marked.

Chapter 5 compared models that differ in many things at once. This chapter changes one symmetry at a time inside one model, and compares each outcome with what Chapter 3 predicts. The predictions, in the order of the sections:

1. A symmetry the data has merges inputs that need the same answer. A model with it built in has fewer things to learn, by a factor of up to the size of the group. The gain should be largest when training puzzles are few, grow with the group, and disappear once the training set covers every case by itself.

2. That gain comes from sharing weights. A model that shares them but is not exactly invariant should lose nothing on puzzles like the training ones.

3. Exact invariance matters where the data cannot teach the symmetry: on inputs the training set never contained, and where the generator leaves a gap between what is valid and what it produces. A symmetry of the task in that gap costs nothing on the right solution, and where the rule itself ignores what the symmetry exchanges, it helps that rule even at full data.

4. A symmetry that leads out of the data sets no ceiling. One that maps valid puzzles onto valid puzzles with another answer sets a ceiling that can be counted in advance, and data does not lift it.

Section 6.1. Design.

Each experiment trains the same template twice or more on the same puzzles: with the symmetry, and with it removed in one of the ways of Section 4.5. Labels keep all weights shared and let the model tell the things apart. Separate weights give each component, attribute or value its own copy of every rule. The separate-weight models are run at the same number of weights as the shared model, by narrowing them, so that a difference is not a difference in size. Every run takes the same number of updates.

Section 6.3. The gain is the sharing, not the exact invariance.

Labels remove the invariance and keep the sharing. With random weights, a labelled model changes its output by 0.4 when the components are mirrored. Trained, it does as well as the exactly invariant model:

Section 6.5. A symmetry given up lets a shortcut in.

On rows of 10, exchanging rows 1 and 2 is a symmetry of the task but not of the data: the exchanged puzzle has the same answer and the generator never produces it (Section 4.2). The training data therefore cannot teach it, and a model without it is free to learn something that holds only on what the generator produces.

Copy links show this. They let the model pass a context panel's value straight to the output, which suits Constant and Distribute, whose answers are values of the context. With a gate per panel, the model can tell the panels apart.

In the generated data a Distribute row is the first row moved by one step, so the value that the third row lacks always stands at the same place in the first two, and the gates learned to fetch it from that address. The gain of copy links is real information and not just extra connections: a control with the same links wired to the wrong puzzle's panels gains 1.9 points where the proper links gain 12.4.

A benchmark score, then, does not tell a rule from a regularity of the generator. Building in a symmetry of the task that the generator lacks closes the gap between the two, at a small cost on the benchmark.

Section 6.6. A symmetry of the task that buys accuracy at full data.

On I-RAVEN-X with rows of 10, an Arithmetic row is a sum: its last value is the sum of the nine before it, or its first value is the sum of the nine after it. The middle columns are interchangeable operands. Exchanging two of them keeps the row valid with the same answer, and it keeps the answer of a Distribute row too, which is the value the row lacks whatever the order. But the generator never produces most of the exchanged rows: it draws the operands one after another from a shrinking range and places the zeros among them in a fixed pattern (Section 3.5). So the exchange is a symmetry of the task outside the support of the data, like the exchange of rows in Section 6.5.

The plain facet model names every column and gives each of the ten its own weights. Column roles name only the first and the last column and read the eight middle columns through one rule, with the panel as a variable (Section 3.3). Any exchange of middle columns is then built in. Progression rows do not survive the exchange, but their answer can still be computed from the first value and the middle values taken together, so the symmetry costs Progression no answer. Prediction, written before the run: Arithmetic improves, and nothing else loses.

(A table is left out here.)

Arithmetic's exact score doubles, as predicted, and Distribute's doubles as well, which fits a rule whose answer does not depend on the order. On the test range, scored once at the end, the gain is smaller: Arithmetic 36.0 ± 2.1 against 20.9 ± 0.5, an increase of 1.7 times rather than 1.9, while matched (93.7, 94.5, 94.7 against 87.1 ± 2.0) and Distribute (21.6 against 10.5) hold (Appendix D.6). The drop is within the sampling error of the 507 Arithmetic rows that all three seeds share, but it is the direction a choice made with those puzzles in view would leave, and column roles were chosen that way, so the test number is the safer one to quote. Progression and Constant move within the spread of the seeds. The named model loses 16 points when two middle columns are exchanged: it reads the order that the generator imposes. The roles model also has a fifth of the weights, so the size of the model could be the cause. It is not: at equal size, with widths 64, 144 and 288 and 1,390k weights, the roles model matches 94.9 (one run).

This is the largest gain from a symmetry at 6,000 puzzles in this chapter, 6.8 ± 1.6 points per seed. The data could not have taught it, because the exchanged orders never occur. Arithmetic stays the weak rule all the same: fewer than half of its values are generated exactly.

Section 6.7. A wrong symmetry.

Two symmetries the data does not have were built into the facet model, exactly, on I-RAVEN-X with rows of 3 and values 0–10. The predictions were written down before the runs.

Columns 1 and 2 tied: out of the data. The model cannot tell (a, b, c) from (b, a, c). For Constant and Distribute rows, and for sums, the exchanged row is valid with the same answer, so for them the tie is a correct symmetry. For Progression and for differences the exchanged row never occurs. Nowhere does the tie ask for two different answers to one input. Prediction: no ceiling; at most a cost in data.

(A table is left out here.)

No ceiling appears: the tied model matches 98.6% at 6,000 puzzles, and Progression, whose rows the tie breaks, is generated exactly every time. A puzzle and its reflection are then one input, both occur equally often, and their answers differ unless the answer is the middle value. Chapter 7. One model, two rules: sum and midpoint.

Cells are exact % on the tested rule, on unseen puzzles, about 500 rows per cell. Sum and midpoint at 100 and 600 puzzles have three seeds for the shared, per-value and plus models, and so do the models of Section 7.7; every other cell is one run, so roughly ±4 points.

Chapter 6 found that a symmetry saves data when the data has it and caps the model when it conflicts. Those were different symmetries in different models. This chapter holds the model fixed and changes only the rule, with two rules built to be alike in everything but one property. The one built-in symmetry then saves most of the data on one rule and caps the model on the other, and both amounts are computed before training.

The question came from the benchmark. Arithmetic is the rule every model that learns from data finds hardest on I-RAVEN-X (Section 5.7). Is that because a sum is a hard function, because its table is large, or because the models lack the right symmetry for it?

Section 7.1. Two rules of one form.

Take a row (a, b, c) of values between 0 and 50.

(A table is left out here.)

Both are rules of the form c = pa + qb. Both have a table of about 1,250 rows, and both are symmetric in their operands. They differ in one algebraic property. Adding the same number s to every value turns (a, b, c) into (a + s, b + s, c + s), and the rule c = pa + qb still holds for it exactly when p + q = 1 (Section 2.4). Midpoint has p + q = 1, sum has p + q = 2. In words: midpoint needs only the differences between values, and sum needs to know where zero is. Of the benchmark's rules, Constant, Progression and Distribute Three survive the common shift as well; Arithmetic is the only one that does not.

It would be wrong to say that sum has no symmetry. The rows of any rule c = pa + qb stay valid under the moves (s₁, s₂, p times s₁ + q times s₂): for sum, one operand may move if the answer moves with it. The two rules have equally many such moves. What separates them is whether the common shift (s, s, s) is among them.

The puzzles. We generated I-RAVEN-X puzzles with rows of 3 and values 0–50 in which Arithmetic is replaced by a test rule, and everything else is kept: the other three rules, the attributes, the splits. Two further rules guard the comparison. Mirror, c = 2a − b, uses midpoint's triples with the columns moved; it keeps the common shift, and unlike midpoint its answer lies outside its operands, as a sum's does. Lookup, b = g(a) and c = g(b) for a fixed random table g, has no symmetry at all and only 51 rows.

Section 7.2. The models.

Shared. The value-axis template of Section 4.5 with a kernel over the whole range, closed into a ring so that the ends of the range are not special, and without plus. It has 14,785 weights and is exactly equivariant to the common shift: with random weights, shifting the input moves the output by the same amount to 7 times 10⁻¹⁷.

Per value. The same template with a separate copy of every rule for each value: 459,635 weights and no symmetry on the values.

The shared model's forward pass. For one attribute, x_p is panel p's value and v runs over the ring of 101 values. The kernel has one learned vector w_k for each offset k from −50 to 50. A row ρ is read through its pairs of neighbouring panels (p, q); (p_a, p_b) is the pair of the question row, and rows 1 and 2 are the complete ones. For a row, π_ρ(v) counts its panels at value v, δ_ρ is its number of distinct values, and κ_ρ the number of values a complete row shares with the question row. Capital letters are learned matrices, a_i and b_i learned vectors, n a learned vector that gives each value its score:

Nothing on the right of these equations depends on v except through v - x_p and the counts at v, so shifting every value moves P with it: that is the symmetry. It also shows Statement 5's point. The score of v reads v's offsets to the two panels of the question row and a descriptor c' that is the same for every v; no term measures from a panel of the question row to a panel of another row. The variants change one line each. Per value, w and every matrix that meets a value are indexed by v as well. Two further models have no value symmetry: the facet model, and a learner that only memorises the rows it has been asked about and is otherwise untrained.

What these models share: a value is a symbol. In the two value-axis models a value is a constant on an ordered axis. In the facet model it is a code that carries the order. The memorising learner treats values as labels. None of them is told that the tested rule is linear in its operands, and none computes with values as numbers. This is the setting of the thesis: a template knows a constant only through the facts about it, and the question is what each of those facts is worth. It is not the only setting. NVSA and ARLC make addition an operation of the representation (Section 2.5), and with values read as numbers the twins need no training at all. Section 7.8 measures that and places it at the end of the same scale.

Section 7.3. Predictions.

A learner that answers from the question row and is equivariant to the common shift must answer (a + s, b + s) with its answer to (a, b) plus s. It can therefore only compute c = b + g(b − a) for some function g of the first step b − a. All rows with the same first step form one orbit, and the learner has one number to learn per orbit (Section 3.6, Statements 2 to 4).

The shared model sees the whole puzzle, and its symmetry is the shift of all nine panels together. That symmetry alone would not cap sum: c₃ = b₃ + (c₁ − b₁) + (a₃ − a₁) uses only differences and is right on every sum puzzle, because a complete sum row shows where zero is (Statement 5). The predictions below apply to the shared model because of how it reads the puzzle. It scores a candidate value by its offsets to the two panels of the question row, by how many panels of each row sit exactly at that value, and by a descriptor that is the same for every value. It never measures from a panel of the question row to a panel of another row. Apart from values that coincide between rows, it is a learner of the form above.

Midpoint. The answer is c = b − (b − a)/2, which has this form. The 1,250 rows fall into 50 orbits, one for each even first step from −50 to 50 except 0. A model with the shift has 50 things to learn where a model without it has 1,250. A learner that does nothing but keep one answer per orbit, fitted on the first 100 training puzzles, has seen 32 of the 50 first steps, and these cover 80% of the unseen rows.

Sum. The answer is c = b + a, and a is not a function of b − a. Within an orbit every row needs a different g, and the model has one. It can be right on one row of each orbit. Sum's 1,225 rows fall into 97 orbits, so at most 97 of 1,225 rows can be right: 7.9%, whatever the amount of data. This is the bound of Wang et al. (2023a, Theorem 4.3) for incorrect equivariance, evaluated on sum's table (Section 3.6.3).

Without the symmetry the two rules should cost the same, since they differ in nothing else. The memorising learner confirms that the tables are equally hard to cover: it gets 4.7 / 24.1 / 90.7 on sum and 5.0 / 21.6 / 90.5 on midpoint at 100 / 600 / 6,000 puzzles.

These are the numbers a counting learner reaches. The claim would be refuted if sharing did not help midpoint, if the models without the symmetry found midpoint much easier than sum, or if lookup were as hard as sum for them.

Section 7.5. What the network generalises is what its orbits allow.

The shared network reaches 60.3 / 91.0 / 92.7 on midpoint where the counting learner reaches 80.0 / 99.8 / 100. Part of the distance has a plain cause: the counting learner is told which rule a row follows, and the network is not. A midpoint row whose first step is ±2 looks, from the question row alone, like a Progression row. At 6,000 puzzles the network gets none of those 33 rows right and 469 of the other 472.

The more telling comparison is on rows whose first step never occurred in a training question. The checkpoints are those of the seeded table in Section 7.4, seeds 1 / 2 / 3.

(A table is left out here.)

Not one row with an unseen first step is answered correctly, at either size or any seed. This is what the model's parameterisation implies. The answer is linear in the first step, c = b − (b − a)/2, but the kernel has one free weight vector per offset and nothing ties the vectors of two offsets together, so the template has no way to carry "half the step back" from the steps it has seen to one it has not (Statement 11 holds for it nearly as it stands). The model fills the orbits the convolution gives it, one at a time, as the counting learner does, only less efficiently. Weights per value do the same one level down: at 100 puzzles they get 20 to 24 of the 25 rows whose pair (a, b) they have seen and 3 or 4 of the other 480. In both models, what generalises is exactly what the built-in symmetry merges. The result is not a finding about neural networks in general, which with a smooth kernel or numbers as inputs could do otherwise. Its use is that the count says, before training, which rows a model of this family will get.

How many training rows an orbit needs can be read from the same checkpoints. Pooled over the three seeds, an unseen row with exactly one training question in its orbit is answered correctly in 422 of 573 cases at 100 puzzles (74%) and in 71 of 72 at 600 (99%). With three or four training questions it is 92% at 100 puzzles. One row per orbit is thus enough once the rest of the network has been trained, which is what the count assumes. The pairs that weights per value get right without having seen them, 11 of 1,440 over the three seeds, are the rate of guessing among 101 values.

Section 7.6. The reversal: giving the model the relation sum needs.

If the argument is right, sum should become as cheap as midpoint in a model that has sum's own moves. The move "one operand and the answer together" is what a relation plus(U, W, V) for U + W = V expresses when it is supplied as background facts: (u + s) + w = v + s. We added these facts to the shared model, with learned rules that walk through them (Section 4.5), and removed nothing else. The model has 19,937 weights. Prediction from the counting learner: with sum's moves, sum is learned from the first few puzzles; midpoint keeps what the convolution gives it.

(A table is left out here.)

The model that was held under 8% learns sum from 100 puzzles. Midpoint is where it was: the differences of 3.6 and 0.7 points are inside the spread of the seeds, so the added facts neither help nor hurt it.

How much this gives away has to be said plainly. A function that respects every one of sum's moves is a + b + k for a single number k. Sum's full group of moves is thus the rule itself up to one constant, and the counting learner with these moves has a single orbit and is right after ten puzzles. The convolution gave midpoint one of the two directions of its group, which left 50 orbits to learn; plus gives sum everything, which leaves one. That is why 98.2 is above 60.3, and it does not mean sum is the easier rule. plus is the relation handed over as background knowledge. What the network learns is when to use it and how to read the result. Learning sum then becomes choosing and reading a given relation, much as in models with programmed rules, PrAE (Zhang et al., 2021) and ARLC (Camposampiero et al., 2024). Section 7.7. A second symmetry: scaling.

Section 7.5 ended on a claim that can be tested: what generalises is what the built-in symmetry merges. If that is right, a model with a second symmetry of midpoint must answer rows with unseen first steps, in the orbits the new symmetry merges and in no others. Midpoint has such a symmetry. Multiply every value by the same number k and the row stays valid: ka + kb = 2kc. In the terms of Section 2.4, the shift makes the zero of the value scale a convention, and scaling makes its unit a convention too: together they are Stevens's interval scale, except that on the ring a scaling may also be negative, which his interval scale, being ordered, excludes.

The model. The shared model's kernel has one weight per offset between two values. The new kernel counts offsets in each row's own unit: a value V lies k units from a value U, V = U + k times S, with one weight per k. The unit S is a constant like the values, and the template never names it. Background facts say which step is k units of S on the model's ring of 101 values. Since 101 is prime, every step has every multiple and every fraction on the ring, half a step included. A rule derives a row's unit from its first two panels, in one of two ways.

Doubled. The unit is the largest power of two in the row's first step. The model is equivariant to doubling all values, and first steps that differ by a power of two fall into one orbit: 26 orbits in place of 50.

Scaled. The unit is the first step itself. The model is equivariant to v to kv + s for every k and s, and all first steps are one orbit. It can only answer c = b + g times (b − a) for a number g it has to learn. For midpoint g is −1/2. As plus does for sum (Section 7.6), this hands over the rule up to one number.

Both have the shared model's 14,785 weights. With random weights, the output of a transformed puzzle equals the transformed output to 2 times 10⁻¹⁷ on 100 puzzles, for every k and s in the scaled model and for doubling in the doubled one wherever the doubled steps stay in range. These are exact on the ring; on the values 0 to 50 a scaling sends most rows out of range, and Section 3.7.1 says what that leaves. The shared model has neither symmetry (a difference of 2 times 10⁻²).

What the ladder says.

Each rung is a piece of knowledge about numbers. That values lie on a line (the shift), that the line has no fixed unit (scaling), that values can be added (plus), that the rule is linear. Each has a price in training puzzles, and the count gives that price before training.

The trained models end in the count's order, at the second attempt. The count says every rung needs less data than the one below it, and from 100 puzzles the networks reach 5.0, 59.9, 89.2 and 100.0%. They stay under the count where many orbits are left: by 20 points with the shift, by 5 with doubling, by nothing with scaling. The order held only once the model could read the rule from the complete rows. With the first row descriptor the two scaling rungs fell below the rung beneath them (Section 7.7), with the same groups and the same counts. The count bounds what a model of this family can reach and orders the rungs; how close a network comes depends also on how it describes a row, and the 20 points with the shift alone are not explained.

A prior and the answer are two ends of one scale. At the top nothing is left to learn across puzzles, and at one orbit a single number is. What is left to learn, counted in orbits, is a unit on that scale. Chollet (2019, pp. 1 and 43) asks that skill be reported together with the priors and the experience behind it (Section 2.4). For one rule and one family of models, the amount of prior can here be counted.

The top rung has its price too. The linear form gives nothing on Distribute Three and on lookup, as the shift gives nothing on sum. The worth of a prior belongs to the pair of model and rule at every rung.

Which rung a model should start from is not something the count decides. Models in which addition is an operation of the representation start from the upper rungs (Section 2.5). A template that treats values as constants starts at the bottom, and this chapter measured what each step up costs it.

Section 7.9. What the experiment shows, and its limits.

What it shows. A symmetry prior is not good or bad in itself. The same convolution is worth an order of magnitude of data on midpoint and costs nearly everything on sum. Its worth belongs to the pair of model and rule, and for this pair it was computed before training: 50 orbits in place of 1,250 rows on one side, a ceiling of 7.9% on the other. The trained networks sat at the ceiling, came close to the saving (59.9% against the count's 74.8% at 100 puzzles, 91.0% against 98.7% at 600, and 98.3% once the row descriptor read the steps), and, as their weights per offset imply, got no row the count did not allow. Section 7.8 puts the same statement on one scale: from weights per value to the linear form, each piece of built-in knowledge leaves less to learn, down to nothing.

Objections.

"Read the values as numbers and both rules are trivial." They are: a linear fit on a puzzle's two complete rows solves them with no training (Section 7.8). That model is given the form of the rule. The twins test what a model pays that is not given it, which is the position of every template in this thesis, and of any model on a rule that is not linear, where the numbers do not help.

"A shift-equivariant model cannot fit a rule that is not shift-equivariant. That is obvious." For a model that answers from the question row it is, and Section 7.4 says so. What is not obvious is that the two rules are otherwise equally hard, and that the size of the gain on midpoint, and which rows it covers, are given by a count.

"Your model sees the complete rows, and from them a shift-equivariant function can compute a sum." It can (Section 7.3). The ceiling holds for this template because it never measures from one row to another, and Section 7.3 says that this is a property of the template and not of the shift.

"Midpoint is not a rule of the benchmark." It is a probe, built to isolate one property. The evidence on the benchmark is Section 7.6 and the value-shift ablation of Section 6.2.

"These are not groups on the values 0 to 50." Correct. A shift can leave the range, midpoint needs a + b even, and the shared model's ring wraps around where the data does not. The orbit counts were made on the actual tables, so the numbers stand; the group language is exact only away from the ends of the range.

Limits.

The test range came last. Every checkpoint of this chapter was scored once on puzzles 8000 to 9999 at the end of the work, with 963 midpoint and 1,014 sum rows (Appendix D.6). The seeded cells move by at most 3.2 points, each within the sampling error of the 500 rows reported here. Every sum mean stays at or under its ceiling within sampling error, and the ladder keeps its order: with the steps at 100 puzzles, 59.4, 88.2 and 100 against the counts 80.0, 94.3 and 100. The facet model on sum at 600 puzzles moves the most, from 31.2 to 24.7, so its lead over midpoint there shrinks from 7 points to 3. The cells at 6,000 puzzles of the shared and per-value models cannot be tested as reported, because their runs saved another epoch than the one the table reads.

Sum and midpoint at 100 and 600 puzzles have three seeds, with standard deviations up to 3.9 points. Three runs fix a standard deviation only to about half its size. The cells at 6,000 puzzles, mirror, lookup and the facet model are one run of about 500 rows, so ±4 points. The effects reported are 50 points and more; differences of a few points between cells mean nothing.

The epoch is picked by exact score on the selection puzzles here, because matched is near 100% for every model on these puzzles and picks arbitrary epochs. The conclusion is the same under either choice. For sum in the shared model the choice is arbitrary under either: the whole-puzzle score does not move with a rule the model cannot learn, and one seed's cell at 100 puzzles (1.8%) is such an epoch.

The runs at 6,000 puzzles with weights per value and the facet model were still improving when they stopped. The comparisons at 100 and 600 puzzles are the firm ones.

The experiment is on rows of 3. That Arithmetic on rows of 10 is hard for the models of Chapter 5 for the same reason is an inference: their table there has billions of rows and they have no symmetry that collapses it.

Every trained model here treats values as symbols (Section 7.2). The comparison between sum and midpoint is a statement about such models; with values as numbers and the linear form given, neither rule needs training.

The ladder of Section 7.8 has three seeds per rung, and its three rungs with a symmetry use the row descriptor that was repaired after the first one failed. It is a second attempt, with the repair predicted before it was run. The first attempt shows that, for one group and one orbit count, the way a model describes a row can decide between 20% and 100%. The count predicts the order and the reach of the rungs, not the accuracy of a given network.

Chapter 8. Discussion and conclusion.

Section 8.1. What a symmetry is worth.

The thesis asked what a built-in symmetry is worth to a relational model, and whether that can be known before training. The answer the experiments give is a count with two sides and a condition.

Where the rule gives corresponding answers on the inputs a symmetry merges, the symmetry is worth the data it lets the model reuse. Where it does not, it is worth a ceiling. Both are numbers of orbits on the rule's table, and both were written down before the runs. The saving matters most in practice, because it is largest exactly where data is scarce and fades as data grows: tens of points at 100 to 600 training puzzles, a few at 6,000 (Section 6.2). Counted in puzzles rather than points, it is the size of the group the data really has. For the attributes that is two to three, not the three of the group the model was given, because type never carries Arithmetic; the runs needed that much data. The ceiling showed up twice: a reflection held a model near half of all values (Section 6.7), and the shift held one at 7% on sums against the 7.9% counted (Section 7.4).

The condition is on the learner. The count describes a learner that shares nothing across orbits and can tell every orbit apart. Our value models share nothing across orbits by construction, so they got the rows the count allows and none of the others (Section 7.5); that part follows from their weights. Whether a network also tells the orbits apart depends on how it reads the puzzle. With the first row descriptor the same group and the same count gave 20% or 100% (Section 7.7), and with the shift alone the network stays 20 points under the count at 100 puzzles, for a reason we did not find. The ceiling has a condition of the same kind: it belongs to the shift together with reading the answer from the question row, not to the shift alone (Section 3.6.3). The count bounds what a model of a given family can reach and orders the models by the data they need. It does not by itself give a network's accuracy.

So the answer to the second half of the question is yes, to the extent that the rule's table is known and the way the model reads the puzzle is part of what is counted. For a generated benchmark both are known. For a task where they are not, the count still says what to look for: the worth of a symmetry prior is a property of the pair of model and task, and a prior that helps on one rule can cap the model on its neighbour.

Section 8.2. Which symmetry, not how much.

It is tempting to summarise Chapter 7 as "Arithmetic is hard because it has no symmetry". That is false, and the difference matters. Sum has as many moves that keep its rows valid as midpoint has. What it lacks is one particular move, the common shift, and that happens to be the move a convolution along the values builds in. Given its own moves, sum is the easier rule of the two.

The practical reading is that "more equivariance" is not a direction in which models improve. The convolution is the natural symmetry to put on a line of values, three of the benchmark's four rules have it, and on the fourth it turns a model that would have learned slowly into one that cannot learn at all. A designer who adds a symmetry is betting that every rule in the task shares it. The bet can be checked beforehand, by transforming valid examples and asking whether they stay valid and whether the answer moves with them (Section 3.5), and the stakes can be counted.

The same holds in the other direction. Midpoint keeps the shift and also scaling, and a model given both needs less data at every step: from 59.9% with the shift alone to 100% with scaling at 100 puzzles, once its row descriptor could read the steps of the complete rows (Sections 7.7 and 7.8). More of a rule's own symmetry helps; a symmetry the rule lacks caps.

Section 8.3. Sharing and invariance are two things.

Building a symmetry in gives a model two properties at once: its weights are shared across the things the symmetry exchanges, and its output is exactly invariant. The label experiments separate them. A model that shares its weights and is not invariant learns as fast as the invariant model on puzzles like the training ones (Section 6.3). It fails where the training data stops: on a rule never seen on an attribute it loses 44 points where the invariant model loses 5 (Section 6.4), and where the generator leaves room, it learns the generator's regularity in place of the rule (Section 6.5). A symmetry in that room can also pay on the benchmark itself: the order of Arithmetic's operands, which the generator never varies, adds 7 points at 6,000 puzzles and doubles the exact sums (Section 6.6).

Sharing, then, is what saves data, and invariance is what carries the model beyond its data. For a benchmark score only the first is needed, which is one reason a benchmark score says little about the second.

Section 8.4. Where the priors came from.

Every prior in this thesis was chosen by someone who had read the generator. The first objection to all of the above is therefore that the answer key was copied into the model. Chollet (2019) gives the terms for an honest reply. Skill can be bought with priors, and "unlimited priors allow system developers to 'buy' unbounded performance on any given task" (p. 25). A fair test may assume a short list of them, including objects, elementary geometry, and numbers, which "may be added or subtracted" (p. 26), with quantities "smaller than approximately 10" in his own benchmark (p. 49). And generating every task from one program is "a weakness", since reverse-engineering the program would solve them all (p. 51). RAVEN is generated from one program.

We sort our priors by whether a person would bring them to any test of this kind, or whether they are read off this generator.

(A table is left out here.)

Read by kind, the prices are suggestive. The largest gains belong to general priors: the shift of values (61 points at 100 puzzles), addition (sum from 5% to 98%), and a graph with a node per attribute (86% against 30% at 300 puzzles, Section 5.4). The generator's priors bought less, and what they bought was gone at full data: 14 points for the components at 100 puzzles, none at 6,000. This is a comparison across different models and datasets, and we do not lean on it.

One generator prior is in every model here, ours and the baselines alike: that rules run along rows. Transposing an I-RAVEN matrix leaves 3% of Progression instances valid (Section 3.5), so a model with rows as facts should fail on a transposed puzzle that a person would not notice had changed. It does. On transposed puzzles the facet model, the model with copy links and the row fold all fall from 95.8–97.4% to 45.0–45.1% matched, and to about 4% exact. RAVEN's rules applied along rows keep 47.7% of the same kind of puzzles, so the models fail as a perfect row-rule solver fails (Figure 8.1 shows a transposed puzzle). This is the prior's cost away from the generator; its worth in training data was not measured. It limits what every score in this thesis means: accuracy on the generator's own distribution, bought partly with the generator's own structure. Nothing here is a claim about the tests people take.

Section 8.5. What the lifted template contributes.

Not accuracy. A graph network in PyTorch with the same symmetries is within half a point of the facet model on I-RAVEN (Section 5.5). The gains reported in this thesis belong to the symmetries and to the graph.

A uniform way to state and prove a symmetry. In the template, a symmetry is the absence of a name. One theorem covers every case, and each was confirmed on random weights to machine precision. The baseline's symmetries were engineered into its node features one at a time, and each needed its own check. The ablations of Chapter 6 are changes of a line in the template: add a fact that labels a component, or write a rule once per attribute. This is a difference in uniformity and convenience, not in what can be built: in a tensor framework, sharing weights over a set or a typed axis is also one line, and equivariant layers come with their own proofs.

Relations of more than two things, used by learned rules. plus(U, W, V) joins three values. In the template it is a fact like any other, and rules with weights walk through it (Section 7.6). A graph network that passes messages along pairs needs extra nodes for the triples to do the same, as networks on hypergraphs or factor graphs have; in the template no change of architecture is needed.

Recursion. The plus facts need not be given as a table. Two rules without weights derive them from the zero and the successor of each value, u + 0 = u and u + (w + 1) = (u + w) + 1, and the model's outputs are then identical to those with the table, to 2 times 10⁻¹⁶. The recursive rule is itself sum's symmetry: move one operand by one and the answer moves by one. All that a model needs to know about addition thus fits in a zero, a successor and one rule. It is written in and not learned, since weights cannot change which facts are derived, and it costs 22 times the grounding time on rows of 10.

A cost. Every puzzle becomes its own network, kept in memory, and trained one at a time on CPU: over 300 GB and about 15 hours for a run on all of I-RAVEN, where the PyTorch baselines need one GPU and a few gigabytes (Section 5.8). Background facts cost too: the plus facts grow with the square of the value range.

Section 8.6. Limits.

One seed. Most numbers are a single training run. Where two seeds exist they differ by 1 to 2 points. Sum and midpoint at 100 and 600 puzzles have three, with standard deviations of 0.4 to 3.9 points for the models of Sections 7.4 to 7.6. So do Chapter 6's comparisons with little data and the attributes at 6,000: standard deviations of 0.2 to 2.7 points, and each seed's gain within 2 points of the mean gain (+14.2, +13.5, +6.3). The vision transformer varies more: its gain from the exchange of rows is 8.6 to 28.0 points across three seeds. The first scaled model of Section 7.7 had two outcomes, 100% at one seed and about 20% at two, which went with whether its row descriptor carried the rule; with a descriptor that reads the steps it is at 100% in six runs of six. The conclusions rest on effects of 10 to 60 points and on their direction, not on differences of a point or two; the 0.4 points between the facet model and its PyTorch counterpart is not a result.

The value axis is given the rules' primitives. Its results are comparisons with itself. The symmetry on values, the centre of Chapters 6 and 7, has not been built into a model that learns the rules from codes. With plus given, learning sum is choosing a given relation, as in models with programmed rules, and the facts grow with the square of the range: the value axis was not run on values up to 1,000.

One object per panel on I-RAVEN-X, and rows of 3 for the twins. The orbit count was tested on tables of about 1,250 rows. That it explains the difficulty of Arithmetic on rows of 10 is an inference.

The count needs the rule's table, and describes one kind of learner. It was made on finite tables, where the groups hold only away from the ends of the range, and on a ring of 101 values where they hold exactly. It bounds a learner that shares nothing across orbits; how close a network comes depends on how it reads the puzzle (Section 8.1).

Rows of 10 are not compared across model families. The runs differ in training length and recipe (Section 5.7).

Perception is trained apart, with labelled objects, and the reasoning model cannot take graded inputs as it stands (Section 5.3). The pipeline is end to end at test time only, and its labels give it more supervision than the black-box transformer gets. A black-box reasoner given the same labels closes most of that gap only from 3,000 puzzles per layout (Section 5.4).

The fixed encoding. Thermometer codes tie the facet model to the value range it was trained on, and the list of attributes is given.

The I-RAVEN-X test pass came last. The text reports puzzles 6300 to 7299, which were looked at throughout the work. Every final checkpoint of Chapters 5 to 7 was scored once on puzzles 8000 to 9999 only at the end, after an external review asked for it (Appendix D.6). The test numbers agree with the reported ones within sampling error, except the facet model on sum at 600 puzzles, a single run, 24.7 against 31.2. Section 8.7. Future work.

1. Sum from differences, across rows. A complete sum row shows where zero is, because c − b equals a. A function built from differences alone, c₃ = b₃ + (c₁ − b₁) + (a₃ − a₁), is right on every sum puzzle and follows a shift of the whole puzzle (Section 7.3). Our templates never measure from the question row to another row, which is why the shift caps them on sum. A kernel between a panel and the panel above it would give the model those offsets. The question is whether it then learns sum with the shift built in and without the plus facts. (The item that stood here, a row descriptor that reads each pair's step, was built and is Section 7.7's repair: the scaled model then learns midpoint at every seed, so the cost measured there belonged to the descriptor and not to the symmetry. On sum it changes nothing, as predicted.) One caution: the function above chains two offsets, and a template that chains offsets for every answer contains it by construction. The test means something only if the chain is the same for every rule and the model still has to find which offsets to chain.

2. Pricing "rows only". The cost on transposed puzzles is measured (Section 8.4). What remains is its worth in data: replace rows by lines, with rows and columns both given as facts, so that the direction of a rule is inferred per attribute, and compare the two models at a few training sizes.

3. Training perception and reasoning together. The recognizer's output, the probability that a value exceeds each threshold, is already a soft thermometer code. The template has to lose its jump at zero first.

4. The value axis on grids. Position slots as constants with the geometry of the grid as background facts, and the number of objects as a line of its own, where Progression on positions is a cyclic shift.

5. Learning the rule that derives addition. The recursion of Section 8.5 is written by hand. Finding it would be learning the structure of a template, not its weights.

Section 8.8. Conclusion.

A built-in symmetry is worth the orbits it merges. Where the rule respects the symmetry, the model learns one answer per orbit and needs correspondingly less data; where it does not, the model is right on one input per orbit and no data helps. Both can be counted before training. In our experiments the trained networks sat at the counted ceiling, needed the counted amount of extra data where the models were otherwise equal, and came close to the counted saving once they could tell the orbits apart.

In a lifted relational template these symmetries are declared by leaving things unnamed, and hold exactly. A model built this way generates the answers of I-RAVEN without the rules and without the candidates: 97.2% on the test split from object labels and 97.0% end to end behind an object recognizer, against 93.1% for a vision transformer with six times the weights, and 90% against 16% when each layout has 300 training puzzles, end to end as from labels. The recognizer learns from labelled objects and the transformer from the answer alone. Given the same labels, a black-box transformer over the recognizer's output gets 19% from 300 puzzles per layout and needs about ten times the data to reach 90%, so where data is scarce the gap is the structure, not the labels. A graph network with the same symmetries does as well, so the credit goes to the structure and not to the framework.

The assignment asked for symmetry-aware architectures that combine object recognition with priors on how objects compose, built on relational neural networks, and for an improvement over black-box vision. Chapters 3 and 4 give the architectures and Section 4.3 the recognition; Chapter 5 gives the improvement, largest where data is scarce; and Chapters 6 and 7 measure, prior by prior, what was bought and what a wrong choice would have cost.

Appendix A. PyNeuraLogic pitfalls.

All PyNeuraLogic models in this thesis run on PyNeuraLogic 0.9.2, which executes on the Java virtual machine and on CPUs only. The five pitfalls below each cost real debugging time, and none of them produces an error message that points at its cause. Each is given with its symptom, its cause and the fix used in the code. A.6 lists the smaller ones.

A.1 Isomorphic value compression must be off for native training.

Symptom. The symbolic templates, whose rules are given and only their weights learned (Section 5.1), trained badly: training accuracy collapsed, and the scores of some candidates did not move at all during training. Nothing was reported as wrong.

Cause. Settings(iso_value_compression=True), the default, merges neurons whose initial values coincide, as an optimisation for inference. In those models every weight starts at the same value, so the score of a candidate can start out equal to the value of a constant fact. The two neurons are then merged, and the candidate's score stays frozen at that constant for the whole of training. The optimisation is correct for a fixed network and wrong for one whose weights are about to change.

Fix. Every native training call in the code builds the model with

(Template code left out here.)

(core/pynl/bench_hub_grounding.py, value_axis.py, pynl_symbolic.py, facet_stream.py and the scoring scripts). PyNeuraLogic's torch wrapper, neuralogic.nn.torch_function.NeuraLogic, already switches it off, which is why the problem appears only in native training. The comment at the build call in core/pynl/pynl_symbolic.py records how large the collapse was.

A.2 Chain pruning removes the transformation of atoms with a single rule.

Symptom. A nonlinearity declared on an atom, such as (R.rel / 3) | tanh, has no effect. The network trains and its outputs look plausible, but part of it is linear where the template says it is not.

Cause. The default chain_pruning=True removes every atom neuron of the grounded network that has a single incoming rule neuron, and its transformation goes with it. The query atom keeps its own. This acts on the ground network, not on the template text: an atom whose template has two rules, of which only one grounds for a given puzzle, loses its transformation as well. Aggregation does not protect an atom either: one rule averaged over several groundings still feeds the atom through one neuron. All three cases were checked on a toy template, where a tanh declared on a single-rule atom was applied with pruning off and dropped with it on, and kept in both settings once the same sum was split over two rules.

Fix. Turning chain pruning off is one option; on an early template it did not help (FINDINGS 9). The code keeps the default and splits the sum of an atom that needs its nonlinearity over two rules. In the facet template (Appendix C), rel and ans, and relp and ansp in the position channel, are written this way, which the code explains in a comment: without these two tanh layers everything after vF would be one linear map, which cannot express that the relation of the complete rows decides how the question row's panels combine. The copy gates and every step of the row fold are split in the same way so that their sigmoid and tanh survive. The other atoms with a single grounded rule keep the declared tanh only on paper: f0, pv1, pv2, ctx, vP and ctxp, and vF for the number attribute, whose second rule has no facets to ground on. Every result in this thesis was obtained with the template as printed in Appendix C and with this behaviour.

A.3 Unweighted selector atoms add 1.0 unless declared hidden.

Symptom. The facet model, trained on all seven layouts, stayed far behind on the 2 by 2 and 3 by 3 grids, although its grid component is built exactly as in the in-out grid layout, where it did well. It placed the objects of every grid on one set of slots, and moving a puzzle's objects to other slots did not change the positions it generated.

Cause. In a lifted relational network an unweighted body atom contributes its value to the rule's sum. A "selector" atom that is meant only to ground the rule, such as obj(P, O) or ocomp(O, C) in the occupancy rule as it was written before the fix (printed with --visible-selectors),

(Template code left out here.)

has the value 1.0, so it adds 1.0 to every component of the result. Each object therefore added its one-hot slot plus 2 in every slot, and the occupancy vector of a panel told the model little more than how many objects it held. This is the semantics of the formalism and not a defect of the library. The attribute channels suffered less, because they average over objects, so there the selectors add a constant instead of an offset that grows with the count.

The weighted fact puzzle(G), which serves as a bias, is unchanged.

A.5 Grounding memory.

Symptom. Large runs die while grounding, before the first epoch (an early run on I-RAVEN-X with rows of 10 and values 0–1000 died at 180 GB). A Jupyter kernel running PyNeuraLogic dies with the default heap of the virtual machine, and CPU-heavy kernels are killed on the login node anyway.

A.6 Smaller pitfalls.

The other pitfalls listed in FINDINGS 5, in brief:

embedding tables must be declared as weighted facts, otherwise the rule silently fails to ground and the error names the query;

every sample needs its own query atom, because samples with the same query are merged without warning;

build_dataset() may be called only once per built model;

Aggregation.CONCAT follows the grounder's order of the constants, not the order in which they were named;

rules with the same head are summed;

Transformation.NORM rescales any non-zero vector to unit length and leaves a zero vector at zero. In the facet model value 0 is the all-zero thermometer code, and since the template has no biases, a value of 1e-6 in its place flips outputs. This is why the facet model cannot take graded inputs from the recognizer without retraining (FINDINGS 39).

Appendix B. Checks on the data.

B.1 The torch trainer reproduces the facet model's targets.

The torch baselines run in another environment (Python 3.7, torch, no PyNeuraLogic), so they cannot call the facet model's code. data_prep/prepare_baseline_data.py therefore writes the facet model's puzzles and splits as plain arrays, and the baselines compute the targets and metrics from these arrays with their own code. To make sure that both compute the same thing, the same script also stores what the facet pipeline itself computes for the first puzzles of every split:

check_target: the facet model's target, facet_target, for the answer panel of each of the first 20 puzzles, per component;

check_spec: the decoded answer, decode(describe(...)), as one integer vector of occupancy bits and value counts per attribute.

Before training, core/baselines/train_baseline.py recomputes both from the arrays for every split and stops unless the targets agree to 1e-6 and the decoded answers are identical. One exception is built in. I-RAVEN-X's Distribute rule draws values up to the maximum inclusive, one more than the codes and counts hold (FINDINGS 20). The code of that value stays distinct, so matching is unaffected, but the facet pipeline's decoder writes the value into the next attribute's counts. Such answers are left out of the decoded comparison, and the baselines score exact generation against the decoded true code. The facet model's own scorer had the same flaw: it capped exact generation at 95.6% on 3 by 3 and at 98.4% on 3 by 10. It was fixed and the I-RAVEN-X checkpoints were rescored on 2026-11-08; the facet model's 3 by 3 exact went from 92.7 to 97.0, and on 3 by 10 nothing moved by more than 0.6. Matching, the choice of epoch and the per-rule exact values were never affected.

The weights are checked in the same spirit. The baselines are sized to the facet model's weight count, which the script reads by building the facet template, and train_baseline.py asserts that every model it builds has the number of weights it was sized to. All 102 torch checkpoints agree with their best.json (RESULTS section 9). For the test pass, core/baselines/test_eval.py checks that the images and the label arrays list the same puzzles, and every checkpoint has to reproduce its recorded validation numbers exactly before it is scored on test once (RESULTS section 1).

B.2 What the generators do to each rule.

Section 3.5 gives, for each transformation of a puzzle, the share of rule instances that still satisfy a rule of the same kind afterwards, measured on 1000 training puzzles per I-RAVEN layout and 6000 per I-RAVEN-X file (analysis/dataset_symmetry_check.py). No instance fails its own label before the transformation. This section adds what the generators' code says about the same transformations, which explains the shares.

Rows 1 and 2 exchanged. In the I-RAVEN generator each row starts from a fresh draw and is built by the same rule objects, and Distribute Three picks its direction with equal probability, so for rows of three the exchange maps one direction onto the other. The exchange is a data symmetry, with one small exception: for Arithmetic on colour the generator redraws row 2 so that plus and minus stay distinguishable. In I-RAVEN-X with rows of 10, Constant, Progression and Arithmetic rows are drawn independently, but a Distribute row is the first row rotated by a constant step. After the exchange no step fits. The exchanged puzzle is therefore never generated, yet its answer, the value that the third row lacks, stays the same: a symmetry of the task but not of the data. With rows of three the exchange survives, because a rotation by three steps leaves a row of three unchanged.

Columns. Progression dies under any exchange of columns, because a Progression row read in another order is not one. Constant survives every exchange, and Distribute survives an exchange of columns 1 and 2 in rows of three but not in rows of 10, where its rows are rotations. Of Arithmetic, what survives is the part that does not depend on the order of the operands: the plus rows under an exchange of columns 1 and 2, and every Arithmetic row of I-RAVEN-X under an exchange of middle columns. The generators do not draw the operands symmetrically, though. I-RAVEN draws the second operand from what the first leaves, and I-RAVEN-X draws its operands one after another from a shrinking range, shuffles them and then moves a zero out of the last operand slot. Most operands are zero, and the share of zeros depends on the column. Operand order is therefore a symmetry of the task but not of the data (Section 6.6).

Values reflected, v mapped to max − v. Constant and Distribute use only equality, and Progression uses order and the successor, so all three keep their rule. Arithmetic uses addition with zero and keeps nothing.

Columns reversed, which moves the missing panel. It maps a Progression step d to −d, plus to minus and one Distribute direction to the other, and it is a data symmetry of both generators on complete matrices. Because it moves the blank, it relates puzzles with different questions, so it is a symmetry for a model that fills any cell, not for ours. Union and difference on positions do not survive it: a union cannot be undone.

Grid positions. Progression on Position moves through the slots in reading order, cyclically, so only the grid maps that keep that cycle keep the rule: on the 2 by 2 grid the left-right and up-down mirrors and the half turn, on the 3 by 3 grid the half turn only. The set rules on positions (Constant, union and difference, Distribute) survive every permutation of the slots.

Attributes. In I-RAVEN-X, size and colour are drawn by the same function with the same rules and range, so exchanging them is an exact data symmetry. Type never carries Arithmetic (B.3), so exchanging type with size or colour holds only away from Arithmetic. I-RAVEN has no exact exchange of attributes: type, size and colour have 5, 6 and 10 values, Arithmetic on size has an extra +1, and type has no Arithmetic.

Components. The left and right components, and the upper and lower ones, have identical constraints and matching rule counts and means, so exchanging them is an exact data symmetry. The inner and outer components of the in-out layouts are not exchangeable: the outer object is always white and larger. Rules act on each component separately, so exchanging components is still a symmetry of the task in every layout.

Nuisance. Angle is drawn at random and no rule governs it (FINDINGS 3). The facts leave it out, so the models are invariant to it by construction.

B.3 Rules per attribute in I-RAVEN-X.

On the I-RAVEN-X training puzzles 0–5999, size and colour each carry the four rules equally often, about 25% each, while type never carries Arithmetic and carries each of the other three rules a third of the time (RESULTS section 7). A recount from the data file for rows of 10 and values 0–50, made for this appendix, agrees. Two consequences are used in the thesis. The attribute symmetry that the facet model builds in is exact between size and colour and holds for type only away from Arithmetic. And when weight sharing across attributes is priced in training puzzles (Section 6.2), the factor counted in advance is 2 on puzzles with Arithmetic, where only size and colour contribute examples, and between 2 and 3 overall; the runs met both counts.

B.4 Other checks.

Three checks were made before any model was trained (FINDINGS 2 and 4, analysis/sanity_checks.py, each with a negative control). I-RAVEN's annotation lists only the occupied slots of a grid, and deriving a slot from an object's index in that list, as the inherited code did, made about a third of the grid puzzles unanswerable; slots are now derived from the bounding boxes. No puzzle occurs in two splits, and the answer index is uniform. A model that sees only the eight candidates is at chance on I-RAVEN, against 90–100% on RAVEN, and the annotation matches the images.

C.1 Notation.

h(X, Y) :- b1, b2. is a rule with head h and body atoms b1, b2. Upper-case arguments are variables, lower-case ones are constants.

{m, n} b(...) multiplies the value of b, a vector of length n, by a learned m by n matrix. A body atom without braces enters with its own value, unweighted.

*b(...) marks a hidden atom: it takes part in grounding but adds nothing to the sum (Appendix A.3).

[aggregation=avg] after a rule says how its groundings for one head are combined; sum is the other choice used here, and the library's default is the average. Rules with the same head are summed.

h/2 [transformation=tanh] sets the activation of every atom of predicate h with two arguments. Under the default chain pruning it is applied only where an atom has more than one grounded rule (Appendix A.2).

[combination=concat] joins the body atoms into one vector instead of summing them; elproduct multiplies them element by element.

The facts of a puzzle are: fv(O, A), the thermometer code of object O's value of attribute A; nm(P, C), the code of the number of objects of component C in panel P; pos(O), the one-hot slot of O; obj(P, O) and ocomp(O, C), which place an object in its panel and component; samepos(O1, O), for two objects in the same slot of two panels of one row; col(P, c), the column of a panel; row(Rw, P1, P2, P3) and full(Rw) for the two complete rows; row3(P1, P2) for the two context panels of the question row; qcomp(G, C), the component that the sample asks for; and puzzle(G), a scalar fact of value 1. Panels are p0 to p7, components k0 and k1, and A ranges over type, size, color and number.

C.3 What each rule does.

The facets, f0 to f2.

f0(O, A) :- {32, 10} fv(O, A) projects each object's code of one attribute to width 32. The rule has the attribute as a variable, so one matrix serves type, size and colour; this is where the attribute symmetry is built in.

pv1(P, C, A) is the summary of a panel's component for the next layer: the average, over the objects of panel P in component C, of their projected facets.

The first f1 rule lets an object read the summary of its own panel and component.

The six f1 rules with samepos let an object read the facets of the objects in the same slot in the other columns of its row, averaged over such partners. Each ordered pair of columns has its own matrix (column 1 reading column 2, column 1 reading column 3, and so on). This is where "the same position means correspondence" and the order of the columns enter. The row is not named, so rows 1, 2 and 3 use the same matrices.

The last f1 rule is the residual. Since the width grows from 32 to 64, it is a learned projection rather than the identity.

f1/2 [transformation=norm] normalises each facet to unit length.

The pv2 and f2 rules repeat the layer, from width 64 to 128.

The value of a component in a panel, pt and vF.

pt(P, C, A) is the average code of the component's objects, unweighted. For a thermometer code this is, bit by bit, the fraction of objects whose value exceeds that bit's threshold, which is the form of the target. For the number attribute, pt(P, C, number) is the code of the object count.

vF(P, C, A) adds a projection of pt to the average of the final facets. Written as two rules, it keeps its tanh for type, size and colour. For number only the first rule grounds, so there it is linear (Appendix A.2).

The rows, rel, ctx and ans.

The two rel rules form the relation of a complete row: each of its three panels enters through the matrix of its column. The sum is split over two rules only so that the tanh survives pruning. The row Rw is a variable, so the two complete rows are read by the same weights.

ctx(C, A) averages the relation over the two complete rows. This is the prior "the same relation holds in every row", and the average makes rows 1 and 2 interchangeable.

ans(C, A) combines the question row's two panels, each through its column's matrix, with the context. It is the model's answer for one attribute of one component.

oa(G, A) decodes the answer to the 10 code bits. The weighted fact puzzle(G) gives each bit a bias of its own: "value greater than k" needs its own threshold for every k, and the template has no other bias. The hidden qcomp(G, C) selects the component that the sample asks for.

The positions, occ to op.

occ(P, C) sums the one-hot slots of a component's objects into its occupancy. This is the rule of Appendix A.3, with the selectors now hidden.

vP, relp, ctxp and ansp give the occupancy the same row structure as the attributes, with widths 32, 64 and 128, and op(G) decodes it to the 22 slots with a bias per slot.

The output, out. The four decoded codes and the occupancy are concatenated and passed through a sigmoid. The loss is the squared error against the description of the true answer's component.

No rule names an object, a complete row or a component; they appear only as variables. Of the attributes, only number is named, by the rule that gives it its count as input, and out names all four to fix the order of the outputs. By the renaming theorem of Section 3.2 the model is therefore blind to reordering objects, to exchanging rows 1 and 2, to exchanging the two components, and to exchanging type, size and colour together with their outputs. The rules do name the columns, c1, c2 and c3, and the question row is set apart by a predicate of its own, row3.

Appendix E. Negative results.

E.1 The row fold does not transfer to other row lengths.

What was tried. Neither column roles nor the row fold (Appendix C.4) name a column by its index, so their weights do not depend on the row length: a checkpoint trained on rows of 10 runs unchanged on rows of 5 or 20, with 288,328 weights for the roles and 304,712 for the fold. The fold seemed the better candidate for transfer, because it applies one learned transition per column, the same at every step, and a toy recursive template trained on rows of 3–5 values had summed rows of 8–10 exactly (FINDINGS 23). Both models were trained on I-RAVEN-X 3 by 10 with values 0–50 and scored on unseen puzzles with rows of 5 and of 20, drawn by IBM's generator (iravenx_task.py --n 5 / 20 --maxval 50 --nconf 0, seed 1234). When neither transferred, both were trained again on rows of 5 and 10 together, 6000 puzzles of each, with the epoch picked on 300 selection puzzles of each. The prediction, written before that run, was that rows of 20 rise well above the fold's 55% if one training length were the cause.

What happened. Neither transferred, after one training length or after two:

(A table is left out here.)

On the lengths it was not trained on, every model stays between 47.7 and 66.3 matched, and only Constant survives. After training on rows of 5 and 10, the exact value per rule on rows of 20 (Constant / Progression / Distribute / Arithmetic) is 99.7 / 2.9 / 11.3 / 4.7 for the fold and 98.3 / 38.6 / 5.8 / 3.9 for the roles. The second training length made rows of 20 slightly worse for the fold (47.7 against 55.1) and did not lift the roles (54.0 against 50.1), so the prediction failed: one training length is not the cause (FINDINGS 33). The fold's Progression, 99.2 exact at the trained length and its best rule (FINDINGS 23), falls to 2.9 on rows of 20. The exact cells on rows of 5 and 20 were scored before the scorer fix of Appendix B.1 and can rise by at most 0.7 and 1.3 points, which changes nothing here.

On I-RAVEN, with rows of three, the fold has nothing to transfer and is below the facet model: 95.8 (63.0) against 97.3 (69.1), with Arithmetic exact at 75.9 against 83.8 (FINDINGS 23). It is therefore used as an I-RAVEN-X model only, where it is the best model on Progression (97.2 ± 1.7 exact over three seeds, D.4).

What it means. Recursion makes transfer to other lengths possible, since the same weights apply at any length, but it does not make it happen. Trained on rows of 10, nothing asks the recurrence to mean the same after 4 steps as after 9, and the readouts learned to decode the state reached after the trained number of steps. Training on two lengths did not change that. The run left two explanations open that it cannot separate: a state carried through up to 19 tanh steps loses precision, and at longer rows the generator's Progression rows start from a narrower range of values, so the values themselves shift. The roles, for their part, are blind to the order of the middle columns but not to their number (FINDINGS 22). After this result the row-length line of work was closed, and the planned test on rows of 100, which was conditional on a transfer, was not run.

E.2 No grokking on Arithmetic.

Why it was tried. On I-RAVEN-X with rows of 10, Arithmetic's exact score stays low in every learned model (D.4). One explanation is that the training sets are too small or too briefly trained, and that a model would find the sum late, after memorising, as in the grokking literature (Power et al. 2022; Nanda et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Varma et al. 2023). The setting most likely to show it is a training set small enough to memorise but above the critical size below which memorising stays the cheaper solution (Varma et al. 2023), many updates, decoupled weight decay, and possibly larger initial weights (Liu et al. 2023).

Setup. 100,000 I-RAVEN-X puzzles with rows of 10 and values 0–50 in which every attribute follows Arithmetic (data_prep/arith_big_data.py), followed by 300 selection and 1000 unseen puzzles of the same kind. Training on these is a diagnostic, not a benchmark result: the rule is known in training and test, so the runs ask whether a model can learn the sum at all, not how it does on Arithmetic among the other rules. Grokking would show as Arithmetic exact high on the training puzzles long before it rises on the unseen ones.

What happened. Arithmetic exact %, type, size and colour pooled, training / unseen (RESULTS section 12).

(A table is left out here.)

No run grokked. The runs fall into three groups. With 1000 or more training puzzles and the recipe of the 100,000-puzzle run, GNN B learns the sum, with training and unseen accuracy rising together, so there is no memorised stage for a late generalisation to follow. In the grokking papers' own setting its training loss rose instead, at every size tried, and nothing was memorised either. With 100 or 300 puzzles GNN B memorises, and the unseen accuracy never comes back in 300,000 steps; at 300 it even reaches 61.7 and then loses it. The fold memorises without decay and with gentle decay: the unseen accuracy peaks near epoch 100 and then sinks, over 400,000 to 600,000 updates. With decay 1 the fold never fits the training puzzles at all. The decay is counted per update, and an update here is one puzzle, so an epoch of 1000 puzzles brings a thousand decay steps, where the grokking papers train on full or large batches with far fewer steps per epoch. The grid of decay values missed the window between memorising and not fitting, and the second round with gentler decay did not find it either (FINDINGS 35).

What it changed. One thing carried over to the benchmark. GNN B at 1 by , trained on the ordinary mixed-rule 3 by 10 puzzles with the recipe that learned the sum here (no dropout, no decay, a constant learning rate, 40,000 steps, two seeds), gets Arithmetic exact 45.1 and 54.0 at the epoch picked on selection, against 22.7 with the regularized setting of D.1 (RESULTS section 5), and 97.9 (45.5) and 98.2 (56.5) on whole puzzles. The 22.7 therefore came from dropout and decay, not from the mixing of rules, and this is the best Arithmetic of any model here that has to learn the sum on 3 by 10 (column roles: 40.5). The regularized setting was chosen on I-RAVEN and holds the torch baselines back on 3 by 10. The other baselines were not rerun without it, and the facet models trained for 60–150 epochs against these 425, so the 3 by 10 comparisons between the facet family and the torch baselines are not like for like (FINDINGS 37, RESULTS section 12).

Appendix F. Reproduction.

F.1 Environment.

PyNeuraLogic models: conda environment pynl with Python 3.11 and PyNeuraLogic 0.9.2, on CPU nodes only, with the Java runtime pinned as in Appendix A.4. Native training always passes iso_value_compression=False (A.1).

Torch baselines, the ViT recognizer and the ResNet-18 frontend: conda environment nvsa with Python 3.7 and torch 1.11, on GPU nodes.

Cluster: SLURM, CPU partitions amd, amdlong and amdfast, GPU partitions amdgpu and amdgpufast. Every job file in jobs/ names its partition, memory and time, and the usage at its top.

Every script runs from the repository root, for example python3 core/pynl/bench_hub_grounding.py --template facet ..., so the data/ and experiments/ paths resolve.

F.3 What a rerun reproduces.

Seeds. The early PyNeuraLogic runs were not seeded, and for the single runs of the ablation table (D.4) the seed was not recorded. A rerun of such a run gives a number within the spread of its seeds, not the same digits. Runs made since --seed was added, and the torch baselines, carry a recorded seed.

The torch baselines' schedule. From 2 to 23 November 2026, after gradient accumulation (--accum) was added, train_baseline.py overwrote the step count that its cosine schedule read with the loss of each batch, so after warm-up the learning rate alternated between its peak and zero and never decayed. Eight runs were trained with the cosine schedule in that time (their best.json has an accum key): the ViT at width 224 and its row-symmetric version on 3 by 10, the two finer-grid ViTs, and seeds 1 and 2 of the ViT with and without rows built in at 3,000 puzzles. They were rerun with the fix, every one scored 2 to 11 points higher, and the tables give the reruns. --schedule alternating reproduces the bug step for step. The runs made before 2 November decayed as the text describes; 99 of them were rerun with the fixed trainer and reproduced.

Checkpoints. A facet checkpoint is template_best.bin with best.json; the template is rebuilt from the arguments stored in best.json, and facet_rule_eval.py rescores any I-RAVEN-X checkpoint that way. A torch checkpoint is model_best.pt with best.json.

The test split. The I-RAVEN checkpoints of D.2 and every final checkpoint of Chapters 5 to 7 (D.6) have had their one test pass, and test_eval.py, facet_rule_eval.py --test and value_axis.py skip or refuse any run that already has a test.json. The I-RAVEN models other than these, the copy-link variants and the row fold on I-RAVEN, and the CNNs were never scored on test. Someone reproducing the work should train a new checkpoint, pick its epoch on the selection puzzles, and score test once.
